How to Use the Google Drive Connector in Muse

1. What does the Google Drive connector do in Muse?

As of October 25, 2026, Google Drive is a live connector in Muse. It was live on launch day, September 8, 2026, and it was still listed in the October 15 in app check, which counted 54 connectors in the web app. There has been no October change to how it works: connect it and Muse can search your files and folders, read what is inside them, and use them as the source for drafts, summaries, and answers. It is a finding and reading connector, not a writing connector, so it is at its best when the question starts with where is that file.

Here is the honest version. The Google Drive connector is the connector for anyone whose work lives in files: contracts, invoices, PDFs, decks, sheets, and the folder structure that made sense three years ago and never did again. The value is not that Muse can open Drive. Any browser can do that. The value is that Muse can reason across your files: find the latest version of the contract, pull the numbers out of a PDF, and tell you which folder the missing document is actually in. Our complete connectors list keeps the dated status of every connector, including which ones are announced but not yet live, so you can check Drive against the rest of the list in one place.

2. What do you need first?

  • Muse access. You need an account with access first, in the United States, Canada, or Japan, where Muse has launched.
  • One Google account. The connector signs in with Google, so use the account that owns the files you actually work with.
  • Your Google sign in. You connect with your Google account, the same way you sign in to Drive itself.
  • Approvals set to ask me. Before you connect anything, set approvals so nothing is moved, shared, or deleted without your tap. This is the single most important step on this page.
  • Google Docs, optional but useful. Drive finds the file and Docs is where the words inside it get worked on. Our Google Docs connector guide covers that half.

3. How do you connect it, step by step?

The connection itself takes a few minutes. The setup around it matters more, so run the steps in order and do not skip the approvals step at the end.

  1. Open Muse and go to Settings. The connectors list lives there. What is printed there is what your account has, because rollout is staged and two accounts can see different lists on the same day.
  2. Find Google Drive in the connectors list and open it. If Drive is not listed, your account has not received it yet. There is nothing to install and nothing you broke. Check again in a few days.
  3. Sign in with Google. Use the Google account that owns the files you want Muse to see. Pick the right one the first time, because switching accounts later means disconnecting and starting over.
  4. Grant the access Muse asks for. Muse needs permission to read your files and folders. Read the permission screen before you accept it. If a scope surprises you, stop and check what it covers before continuing.
  5. Set approvals to ask me. In Muse settings, set approvals so that moving, sharing, or deleting a file always waits for you. This is what stops Muse from ever changing your filing without your say.
  6. Test with a read only request. Ask Muse to find a file you know well and summarize what is inside it. Read the summary against the original file. If it matches, the connection is good. Do not ask it to move or share anything on the first day.
  7. Use it read only for a week. Searches and summaries only, no moves, no shares, no deletes. Once you trust what it finds and how it reads, start approving changes one at a time.

4. What can you actually do with it?

The connector earns its place with six uses. Everything else is a variation on one of them.

Find the file you cannot find

Ask Muse to find the file by what is inside it, not by its name. This beats Drive search when you remember the meaning of a document but not the words in its title, because Muse reads for meaning. Tell it what you remember: the client, the month, the topic, the number in it. The answer should come back with the exact file name, the folder it sits in, and the date it was last changed. If there are several versions, ask which one is the latest and what changed between them. The failure mode to avoid is trusting the first file Muse returns when three files look alike. A contract named final, a contract named final v2, and a contract named final FINAL are three different files, and only one of them is the one that was signed. Ask for the dates, and open the file yourself before you quote anything from it.

Read a PDF and pull out what matters

PDFs are where information goes to hide. Ask Muse to read a PDF in your Drive and pull out the numbers, the dates, and the terms that matter, in plain words. This is the use that saves the most time for business owners, because invoices, contracts, and reports all arrive as PDFs that nobody wants to read twice. The rule to give Muse is simple: quote the number, and tell me which page it came from. A figure with a page number can be checked in seconds. A figure with no source is a guess wearing a suit. Use it on the invoice you need to pay, the contract you need to renew, and the report you need to brief your team on. Then open the PDF once and check the three numbers that matter most before you act on them.

Compare versions of the same document

Ask Muse to read two versions of a document and tell you what changed between them. This is the use for the file that has been edited by four people and renamed twice, where nobody remembers what the last version actually says. Muse reads both files and lists the changes: what was added, what was removed, and what was reworded in a way that changes the meaning. The reworded lines are the ones to read yourself, because a small change in wording can be a big change in what you promised. Use this before you sign anything that has been through more than one round of edits. If the comparison surprises you, stop and read the full document. The comparison is a map, not the territory, and the lines that touch money or deadlines deserve your own eyes.

Organize a messy folder

Ask Muse to look at a folder and propose a structure for it: which files belong together, which ones are duplicates, and which ones can be archived. Do not let it move anything on the first pass. Ask for the plan in chat, read the plan, and approve the moves one folder at a time. This is the use that quietly pays off over months, because a Drive that is organized once stays organized only if the habit sticks. The rule that makes it stick is naming: every new file gets a name that says what it is, who it is for, and when it was made. A file named Q3 report final v2 tells you nothing in six months. A file named Acme invoice March 2026 tells you everything. Muse can propose the names. You have to use them.

Brief yourself from your own files

Ask Muse to read the files for a project, a client, or a meeting and brief you on what you need to know before you walk in. This is the use that turns Drive from storage into memory: the proposal, the last email, the notes from the call, and the numbers from the sheet, all pulled into one short brief. Our Gmail connector guide covers the other half of this habit, because the latest news on a project usually arrives as email first. Put the open questions at the top of the brief, not buried at the bottom. The person who reads it is about to walk into a room, and the first thing they need is what is still undecided and what they are being asked to decide.

Check what is shared and with whom

Ask Muse to list which files in a folder are shared, and who can see them. This is the use nobody thinks about until a client sees a file they were never meant to see. Run it once a month on the folders you share most, and treat the list it returns as your sharing audit. If a file is shared with someone who no longer works on the project, remove them. If a folder is shared with anyone who has the link, ask yourself whether that is still what you want. Sharing is easy to grant and easy to forget, and the files you shared a year ago are still shared today unless someone removed them. A short monthly check is cheaper than a single awkward conversation about a file that should have stayed private.

5. What can it not do?

The limits are honest and worth knowing before you lean on the connector.

  • It cannot see files you never connected. Files in other accounts, files on your computer that were never uploaded, and files in other tools are invisible to it. It reasons from what you connected, nothing more.
  • It cannot guarantee it read a file right. A summary can miss a clause, and a number pulled from a PDF can be the number from the wrong table. Check anything that touches money, legal terms, or a promise to a customer against the original file before you act on it.
  • It cannot fix a Drive with no structure. If every file lives in one folder with names like final and new doc, Muse will find things slowly and organize them badly. The folder structure comes first, and no connector can build it for you without your decisions.
  • It cannot recover a deleted file on its own. Drive keeps deleted files in trash for a limited time, and restoring them is a Drive action you should do yourself, carefully, in Drive. Do not ask Muse to clean up files until you know exactly what trash and restore do in your account.
  • It cannot move, share, or delete a file without your approval when approvals are set right. That is a feature, not a bug. If you turn approvals off, you lose the safety net, so keep them on for anything that changes your filing.

6. What should you watch for?

As of October 25, 2026, Google Drive is a settled connector: live since September 8, still listed in the October 15 in app check, and unchanged through October. There is no October update to how it works, so the habits around it still matter. Check every file Muse finds against Drive itself for the first week, especially when several files have similar names. A wrong version acted on fast is worse than a slow search you verified.

Be careful with shared folders. When a folder is shared with your team or a client, a move Muse makes is a move they feel. Set a rule for yourself: Muse proposes in chat, you read, you approve, and only then does anything move. The moment you start approving moves without reading them is the moment the connector stops being an assistant and starts being a risk. This matters most for folders other people rely on, like client folders and finance folders, where a file in the wrong place can look like a file that does not exist.

Check your data controls. In Muse settings, open Data controls and look at the switch for helping improve the models. If you do not want your chats used for training, turn it off before you start asking Muse to read your files. That switch controls whether Meta may use your chats to train future models.

Finally, remember that Drive is a finding tool, not a writing tool. When you need to work on the words inside a document, that is a Docs job, and the Google Docs connector guide is the one to read. When you need to work on the numbers inside a sheet, that is a Sheets job, and our Google Sheets connector guide covers it. Keep the three connectors straight in your head: Drive is where files get found, Docs is where words get written, Sheets is where numbers get checked. The pairing is what makes each of them useful, and the habit of finding first and working second is what keeps your files from piling up in the wrong folders.

Frequently asked questions

Can Muse search my Google Drive?
Yes. Muse can search your files and folders by name and by what is inside them. Ask for a file by what you remember about it, and Muse will tell you the exact file name, folder, and last changed date.
Can Muse read PDFs in my Drive?
Yes. Muse can read PDFs stored in the Drive account you connect and pull out numbers, dates, and terms. Ask it to quote the page each figure came from, so you can check the important ones yourself.
Can Muse move or delete my files?
It can propose moves, and it can carry them out only with your approval when your approvals are set to ask me. Use it read only for the first week, and approve changes one at a time after that.
Can I use the Google Drive connector outside the United States, Canada, and Japan?
Not yet. Muse itself has launched in the United States, Canada, and Japan only. Join the waitlist and we will send you a fresh code on launch day in your country.
What happens if I disconnect Google Drive?
Muse loses access to your files. Your files stay in Google Drive, and nothing is moved or shared after you disconnect. You can reconnect the same account or a different one later.
Is my file content used to train Muse?
There is a Data controls switch in Muse settings for helping improve the models. If you do not want your chats used for training, turn it off before you start asking Muse to read your files.
