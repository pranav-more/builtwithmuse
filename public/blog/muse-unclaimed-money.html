3. How do unclaimed property databases work

Unclaimed money is ordinary money that went quiet. A bank account with no activity, a final paycheck that was never cashed, a utility deposit that was never returned, an insurance refund, a dividend check, a store credit. After a dormancy period, usually three to five years depending on the state and the type of property, the company that holds it is required by law to report it and hand it to the state. The state does not take ownership. It holds the money in trust until the rightful owner files a claim, and in most states the claim never expires.

Each state runs its own database, usually under the state treasurer or comptroller, and every one of them is free to search. There is no single national database, which is exactly why people miss money: a person who has lived in four states has four separate searches to run, and most people run zero. Two tools close that gap. MissingMoney.com is the multi state search endorsed by NAUPA, the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators, and it queries roughly 40 participating states in one search. For the states that do not feed MissingMoney, the directory at unclaimed.org links straight to each official state program. Federal money sits outside both: the IRS holds undelivered tax refunds, TreasuryDirect holds matured savings bonds, the FDIC holds funds from failed banks, the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation holds old pensions, and HUD holds FHA insurance refunds.

Muse fits this job because the work is a checklist, not a judgment call. It can take your list of states and names, run the searches one by one, read the results, and tell you which hits look like you and which look like a stranger with your name. It can also read the claim form for each hit and tell you what documents that state wants. What it cannot do is prove you are you. The claim itself needs your ID, your proof of address, and sometimes your Social Security number, and that part is yours.

4. The method, step by step

Run the steps in order. The first pass takes under an hour, and the claim paperwork is the slow part, not the search.

  1. List every state and every name. Write down each state where you have lived, worked, gone to college, or held an account, and each version of your name. Give the full list to Muse in one message. A state you forget is money you never search for.
  2. Search MissingMoney first. Ask Muse to search MissingMoney.com for each name and state. This covers roughly 40 states in one pass and is the fastest way to find the big hits.
  3. Search the missing states directly. Ask Muse to use the unclaimed.org directory to find the official program for each state that MissingMoney does not cover, and search those one by one on the official state site. Never use a paid finder site when the official search is free.
  4. Check the federal sources. Ask Muse to check the IRS Where is My Refund tool for undelivered refunds, TreasuryDirect for matured savings bonds, the FDIC for failed bank funds, and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation for old pensions. These are the sources most people never think to check.
  5. Sort real hits from same name strangers. For each hit, ask Muse to compare the address on file and the property type against your history. A hit in a town you never lived in under your name is usually a different person. Do not file a claim on a hit you cannot tie to yourself.
  6. Let Muse read the claim form, then file it yourself. For each real hit, ask Muse to read the official claim page and list exactly which documents that state wants. Then file the claim on the official site yourself, with your own ID and proof of address. Keep a copy of everything you submit and the claim number.
  7. Hunt refunds and duplicate charges in parallel. While the property search runs, ask Muse to scan your email receipts and recent statements for duplicate charges, subscriptions billed twice, and refund windows you missed. A duplicate charge is found money too, and the dispute window is usually 60 days, so this half is more time sensitive than the property half.
  8. Track what you filed. Ask Muse to keep a simple list: property, state, amount if shown, date filed, claim number, and what is still needed. Check the list once a week. States commonly take several weeks to approve a claim, and some ask for one more document before they pay.

6. What to watch for

The search is free, and anyone charging you for it is the first thing to watch for. Official state searches cost nothing, and official claims cost nothing. Paid finder services and text messages claiming the state owes you money are scams. If a site asks for a fee to search, close it and use the official state site or MissingMoney instead. If a message asks for your Social Security number, your bank login, or a payment to release funds, it is a scam, full stop.

Guard your Social Security number even on real sites. A claim form may legitimately ask for it, because the state uses it to confirm you are the owner. Give it only on the official state claim page, never in a chat with a third party service, and never paste it into a prompt you do not need to. Muse does not need your Social Security number to search. It needs your name and your states.

Verify every hit on the official site before you file. Muse can misread a result, match the wrong person with your name, or report an amount the state does not actually show. The official database is the source of truth, and the claim you file should match what it shows, not what a summary told you. The same rule applies to duplicate charges: check the statement yourself before you dispute anything, because a charge that looks duplicated is sometimes a legitimate second installment.

Expect the claim to take weeks, and expect some claims to need more paper. States commonly ask for a photo ID and proof of the address on file, and larger amounts or claims for a deceased relative need more: a death certificate, proof that you are the heir, or estate papers. Claims that need a notarized form or a wet signature cannot be finished by an agent at all. File those by mail and keep copies.

Finally, keep the two kinds of found money separate in your head. Unclaimed property has no deadline in most states, so there is no rush and no reason to pay anyone to hurry it. Refunds and duplicate charges do have deadlines, usually around 60 days from the statement date for a card dispute, so run that half first if your time is short. Frequently asked questions

Is it really free to search for unclaimed money?
Yes. Every official state database is free to search and free to claim from, and MissingMoney is free too. Anyone charging a fee to search is selling you something the state gives away.
How long does a state take to pay a claim?
Most states take several weeks to approve a claim, and some ask for one more document before they pay. A claim that needs a notarized form or a mailed signature takes longer than an online one.
Can Muse file the claim for me?
It can fill in the form and list the documents the state wants, but the filing needs your ID and your approval. Claims that need a signature or a notarized form cannot be finished by an agent at all.
What if someone else has my name?
Check the address on file against your own history before you file. A hit in a town you never lived in is usually a different person. Filing a claim on property that is not yours is fraud, so sort the hits first.
Does unclaimed money expire?
In most states it does not. The state holds it in trust until the rightful owner claims it, so a search this year finds money from a decade ago. A few property types have their own rules, so check the state page for the type you found.
Can I claim money for a relative who died?
Yes, as an heir, but expect more paperwork: a death certificate and proof that you are entitled to the estate. The state page for that property lists exactly what it wants.
What about refunds and duplicate charges?
Ask Muse to scan your receipts and statements for duplicate charges and missed refund windows. Card disputes usually have a window of about 60 days from the statement date, so run that half promptly.
I searched and found nothing. Did I do it wrong?
Probably not. Most people find nothing, and a clean search is still a useful answer. Check again after a move or a job change, and search under past names and misspellings before you conclude there is nothing there.
