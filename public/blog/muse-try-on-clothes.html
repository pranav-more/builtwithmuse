What is Muse Try On?

Try On is Muse's answer to the oldest problem in online shopping: you cannot tell how a piece of clothing looks on you until it arrives. While you shop, Muse offers to show how the item looks on you, built from the exact product listing it found. Not a similar item, not a generic outfit, but the listing you are looking at. The Muse account called it 'the fit check before the fit check', and that phrase is the whole pitch: a quick visual check at the moment you hesitate, before you commit.

Alexandr Wang, Meta's chief AI officer, announced it on X on November 18, 2026, writing that you can now try on clothes in Muse before you buy them. The Muse account posted shortly after with the fit check line. David Singleton, of Meta Superintelligence Labs, explained it across November 18 and 19, posting on November 19 that he had used it to pick a holiday sweater, describing the task as ugly enough to be fun, nice enough to wear all day. The coverage is at iPhone in Canada and All Things Muse.

The one sentence to remember is the limit: it is still a preview of the look, not a promise that the size will fit. Try On shows you the outfit. It does not measure you, and it does not guarantee the garment. That distinction is not a footnote. It is the difference between a useful preview and a misleading one, and the rest of this guide keeps the two apart.

How does Try On work while you shop?

Muse offers the preview while you are shopping. It does not wait for you to ask for it: the agent proactively prompts you while you shop, offering to show how an item might look. You are browsing, Muse finds a piece of clothing, and it offers the preview at the point where shoppers usually hesitate over size, fit and appearance. That timing is the feature. A try on tool you have to go looking for is a chore. One that appears when you are already deciding is a nudge.

Muse finds the products through its retailer connections, or through its own browser when a shop has no direct connection. That means the preview is built from the real listing, with the real product, not a stock photo of a similar item. You can watch what Muse is doing and take over whenever you want. The agent works in the open, and you stay in charge of the session. If the preview looks wrong, or the item is not what you wanted, you say so, and the agent moves on.

What Muse checks, and what checks Muse, is worth one paragraph. Singleton says Muse checks shopping sites and their content, while a separate system called Sentinel reviews Muse's actions before they reach the internet. The two layers are the safety model for shopping: Muse does the finding, Sentinel reviews the doing. Neither layer changes the approval rule, which is covered below, and neither layer turns a preview into a fitting.

What can the preview tell you, and what can it not?

The preview can tell you how the item looks: the colour against your skin, the shape against your frame, the style against the rest of your wardrobe. That is the part of the decision that a flat product photo cannot answer, and it is the part Try On is built for. Choosing between two colours, two patterns, or two styles is a visual decision, and a preview answers it in seconds. That is the real use: not buying with confidence, but choosing with better information.

The preview cannot tell you how the item fits. Size, tightness, comfort, drape, fabric weight, and how the garment moves when you do are all outside what a picture can promise. A preview that looks right can still arrive too tight across the shoulders, too long in the sleeve, or too loose at the waist. No announcement for Try On claims otherwise, and the honest way to use it is to treat the preview as a style check and the retailer's size chart as the fit check. Read the chart, check the measurements, and read the reviews from people with your build before you buy.

The safest way to use Try On is as a filter, not a verdict. Use it to rule out the colour that does not suit you, the pattern that fights the rest of your outfit, and the style that looked better in the photo than on you. Then buy the survivor the ordinary way: size chart, measurements, reviews, and the retailer's return policy. A preview that saves you from one wrong colour is worth having. A preview that talks you into the wrong size is not.

Why is Muse adding features for shoppers?

Because shopping is what Muse is already used for most. On Meta's third quarter earnings call on October 21, 2026, Mark Zuckerberg said more than 10 million people in the US and Canada had set up Muse in about six weeks, and shopping was the standout use, with over 25 percent of weekly active users shopping through it and an average purchase over $200. Those are Meta's figures, and they explain the product direction: the agent's biggest job is already shopping, so the features that help shoppers decide are the features that get built.

Muse could already find an item and check out, including through Shop Pay on Shopify stores. What it could not do was answer the question every shopper asks at the last step: how will this look on me. Try On is that answer, in preview form. It does not change what Muse can buy. It changes the moment before the buy, the hesitation that kills most online clothing orders. A shopper who can see the outfit is a shopper who finishes the order, and a shopper who cannot is a shopper who leaves the tab open and never comes back.

The shopping story around Try On is wider than one feature. Loblaw became Muse's first Canadian retail partner on November 18, 2026, the same day Try On was announced, bringing grocery shopping into Muse in Canada. The ten retailers Meta announced in September are still listed as announced, not live, in trackers checked November 14 and 15, 2026, and Amazon has blocked Muse since September 20. Try On arrives into that mixed picture: the agent can shop, the partners are filing in slowly, and the features that make shopping feel safe are the ones that will decide whether people trust it with their money. The shopping context is covered in the Amazon guide and the Black Friday guide.

Do you still approve every purchase?

Yes, and this is the rule that makes Try On safe to try. Every purchase needs your approval, whatever the price, so liking a preview is not permission to place an order. Muse offers the preview, you look at it, and nothing is bought until you say so. The approval is the whole safety model for shopping with an agent, and it is worth treating as a habit rather than a formality: read the basket, check the store, check the total, and then approve.

The approval rule also answers the worry that a preview will talk you into something. A preview is persuasive by design: it shows you the outfit looking good, because that is its job. The approval step is the counterweight. It forces a pause between liking the look and spending the money, and in that pause you can check the size chart, the measurements and the return policy. Use the pause. The shoppers who get burned by agent shopping are the ones who approve without reading, and the preview makes that temptation stronger, not weaker.

One more habit belongs here: check the store before you buy, not after. Muse finds products through retailer connections or its own browser, and the store behind the listing decides the price, the shipping and the returns. A preview that looks right on a store with a bad return policy is a worse buy than the same item on a store that takes returns freely. The preview tells you about the outfit. The store tells you about the purchase. Read both.

How do you try it yourself?

Shop for clothing in Muse, and watch for the offer. That is the honest instruction, because the announcement gives no setup steps and no setting to enable. Muse proactively offers the preview while you shop, so the way to meet Try On is to shop: ask Muse to find a piece of clothing, a sweater, a jacket, a dress, and see whether it offers to show how it looks on you. If the offer appears, the feature has reached your account. If it does not, the rollout has not reached you yet.

When the offer appears, use it the way the guide above suggests: as a style filter, not a fit verdict. Ask to see the item in a second colour, or against the rest of an outfit, and compare. Then check the size chart and the measurements before you approve anything. If the preview looks wrong in a way that surprises you, say so in the chat, because the agent learns from the correction, and the next preview gets better.

What comes next for shopping with Muse?

Try On is one step in a shopping push that is moving fast. In the same week, Loblaw went live in Canada, the Chrome extension put Muse inside the user's own browser, and Poppy gave Muse a standard way to identify itself to stores. Each piece solves a different part of shopping: Try On answers how it looks, the extension answers how Muse reaches the store, and Poppy answers how the store knows it is you. Read together, they are Meta building the shopping agent in layers, and Try On is the layer the shopper actually sees.

What comes next is likely more of the same: more stores, more countries, and more of the decision covered by the agent. The money cases Meta promotes point the same way: the money challenge guide, at can Muse really find you $1,000, shows Muse finding cheaper insurance, refunds and forgotten subscriptions, and the shopping features are the same idea applied to buying instead of saving. The guide to watch is the one that covers the next store that goes live, because each new store is a new place Try On can work.

The question to keep asking is the one this guide opened with: preview or promise. Every shopping feature Muse adds will be sold as confidence, and the honest test is always the same. Does it tell you something real about the purchase, or does it only make you feel better about it? Try On tells you something real about the look, and nothing about the fit. Use it for the first, check the chart for the second, and you will get the value without the regret.
