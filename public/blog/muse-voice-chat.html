The short answer

Muse Voice is live for every user in the US. Meta rolled it out on November 5, 2026, with real time voice conversations and an expressive avatar, in the Muse app on mobile, web and Mac. Tap the phone icon in the top right corner of a chat and start talking. Muse answers in voice right away, you can interrupt, and the avatar reacts with expressions and gestures that match the tone of the conversation. It is not the same as Muse phone calling, which is Muse dialing a business for you. Voice is US only to start. Meta has named no other countries and no dates for them yet.

This page tracks what is actually known: what Voice is, how it differs from phone calls, who has it, how to start, what the avatar does, whether it needs a paid plan, and what to do while you wait outside the US.

What is Muse voice chat?

At the reveal, Meta presented voice as the natural way to use a personal agent: you speak, Muse answers in voice, and the conversation keeps moving without typing. The point is work that happens while your hands and eyes are busy: driving, cooking, walking, settling a child. A voice conversation also carries the back and forth of a real chat, with follow up questions and corrections mid sentence, rather than one recorded message at a time.

Two nearby features are not voice chat, and naming them clears up half the confusion. Voice notes are recorded clips you send into a chat; Muse listens and replies, usually in text. Dictation is your phone keyboard turning speech into text before Muse ever sees it. Voice chat is the third thing: a live conversation in which Muse itself speaks. When users say voice mode, this is the feature they mean. It is also the feature people picture when they say talking to Muse, which is why its absence is felt more keenly than its presence would be remarkable.

One more piece of context. The models behind Muse are changing at the same time. Meta has said its newest model family, Muse Wonder, will soon power experiences in the Muse app, rolling out first to subscribers. Meta has not said that voice chat is tied to that model rollout, and the two should not be read as the same event. Our Muse Wonder guide tracks the model side of the story, including what subscribers are seeing first.

Is voice chat the same as Muse calling businesses?

No, and this is the mix up that fills the discussion forums. The two features share a microphone and nothing else. One is a way of talking to your own agent. The other is your agent talking to strangers on the phone network.

Voice chat

You talk to Muse. Muse talks back to you. Nobody else is on the line, no phone number is dialed, and the conversation never leaves your Muse app. It is a way of speaking to your own agent, hands free.

Phone calling

Muse talks to a business, on the phone network, for you. You are not part of the conversation. Muse dials a restaurant, a salon, or a support line, handles the call, and reports back with a summary and a transcript.

The status of the two features is different as well. Phone calling went generally available on November 12, 2026 for paid subscribers in the United States, after limited testing behind a request list since September 17, 2026. Voice chat was previewed at Connect on September 23, 2026, reached some users quietly from October 24, and rolled out to every US user on November 5, 2026. If a post says Muse called someone, that is the phone feature. If a post says someone talked with Muse, that is voice chat. Our guide to Muse phone calling covers the calling feature in full: what it does, what real users report, and what it refuses to do.

Who has voice chat today?

Every user in the US. Meta announced the rollout on November 5, 2026, and Alexandr Wang put it plainly the same day: Muse Voice is out for everyone in the US. The rollout covers the Muse app on mobile, the web at muse.ai, and the Mac app. It is US only to start. Meta has not named Canada, Japan, Singapore or Mexico for Voice, and has given no dates for them. Meta has also said Voice will come to AI glasses and to the Muse Charm device later, with the Charm planned before the end of 2026. If you are outside the US, Voice is still coming, with no published schedule.

Before November 5, Voice reached people in drips. Meta previewed real time conversations at Connect on September 23, 2026. From October 24, some users reported the voice option appearing on their accounts with no announcement from Meta. That was a limited, unannounced rollout. November 5 turned it into an official launch for all US users, with the expressive avatar front and centre.

One thing has not changed: there is no switch to turn on and no waitlist for Voice that Meta has documented. If you are in the US and your app is up to date, it is there. Anyone selling early access to Voice is selling something Meta has not announced.

What does the avatar do?

As Muse speaks, the avatar uses small expressions and gestures that match the tone of the conversation. It reacts while you talk, so you can tell it is following along. That is the point of it: not a video of a person, but a face that shows it is listening.

Two limits are worth knowing. The avatar does not see you. It hears you and reacts to what you say, but there is no camera on your side of the call. And the avatar is a character you can change. The default is nicknamed Jolly. You can make it look like a person, an animal, or another character, and you can pick from hundreds of voices, changing the style whenever you want.

While you talk, Muse keeps working. It picks up the thread and works on your request while the conversation goes on, on a walk, making dinner, or wherever you are. That is the difference between Voice and a voice note: the conversation and the work happen at the same time.

Does voice chat need a paid plan?

Meta announced Voice for everyone in the US and stated no subscription condition. Nothing in the announcement ties Voice to Power or Maximum, and no Voice specific price or usage limit has been published. The honest statement is that Meta put it out for every US user with no plan requirement mentioned.

Muse paid plans buy weekly usage capacity, not features. Power costs 20 dollars a month and Maximum costs 100 dollars a month, and both raise how much you can use Muse each week. Voice sits inside that same usage: talking to Muse uses your allowance the way typing does. If you talk to Muse all day, you will reach the limit faster, and a paid plan raises it. That is the only sense in which paying matters for Voice.

What do people want it for?

The discussions are just as clear about the demand as they are about the wait. The first use is the car. Drivers want to talk to Muse the way they talk to a passenger: ask a question, hear the answer, follow up, all without looking at a screen. The October 13 thread about a parent who chats with Muse while driving is full of this, along with a wish that the app would notice motion and restrain itself until the car stops.

The second use is the busy hands hour: cooking, carrying, fixing, walking between meetings. Typing is the tax on every one of those moments, and voice removes it. The third is simpler, and maybe the largest: many people think better out loud. They want to talk an idea through with Muse the way they would with a colleague, hearing answers at speaking pace instead of reading them at screen pace. None of these uses needs Muse to dial anyone. In the US, the wait for voice chat is over; outside the US, all of them are still waiting on a rollout with no published schedule.

How do you start a voice chat?

Update the Muse app to the newest version in your app store. Then open a chat with Muse and tap the phone icon in the top right corner. The conversation starts right away. Talk the way you would to a person in the room. Muse answers in voice, and you can interrupt whenever you need to. There is no wake word and no recording to send first.

Three ways to watch it. Voice only, if you want to keep the screen clear. The avatar alongside the conversation, so you can read while you listen. Or full screen, face to face with the avatar. Pick the view that fits the moment and change it whenever you want.

If the phone icon is not there, check two things. First, the app version, because staged features ride on app updates and an old build can hide a feature your account already has. Second, your country. Voice is US only to start, so outside the US the icon will not appear yet, and nothing is broken on your side.

What can you do while you wait?

If you are outside the US, the wait is still on, and it does not have to be wasted. The habits that make voice chat useful can start today. Send voice notes instead of typing long messages; Muse handles spoken requests well, and you build the habit of talking to it. Use the dictation on your keyboard for long prompts when you are walking. Ask Muse to keep answers short and structured, which matters even more once the answer will be spoken aloud.

You can also prepare the ground the way you would for text. The memory, connectors, and approvals you set up now carry into voice later, and an agent that already knows your calendar and your rules is far more useful spoken to than a stranger is. Setting that groundwork up is covered in our Muse setup guide.

One distinction is worth keeping straight while you experiment. The voice capability that exists today, for the users who have it, is the phone feature: Muse speaking to businesses on calls. If your real need is Muse's voice doing errands, that is the phone calling feature, generally available for paid subscribers in the US since November 12, 2026, and it is separate from voice chat entirely. If your need is hearing Muse answer you, clips and dictation are the bridge, and the live conversation is the destination.

One use case comes up again and again in the discussions: driving. A user posting on October 13, 2026 described a parent who chats with Muse while driving, and wished the app would notice motion and limit itself. Until voice chat and proper driving safeguards arrive, dictation plus short answers is the safer pattern, and anything that needs reading can wait for a stop.

If your interest in voice is really about your business phones, that is a different product category entirely. Voice agents that answer a business line, take bookings, and follow your scripts exist today, from companies built for exactly that job. Our Muse vs ElevenLabs comparison explains who makes your calls, who answers your phone, and how owners combine the two.

What about the do it yourself voice gadgets?

Both voice discussions include the same hobbyist workaround: building a small voice gadget with Meta's open source SDK and a tiny development board, the M5StickS3, so a physical device on your desk can talk to Muse. It is a maker project, not a product. It needs building, it will not behave like the finished feature, and Meta does not support it as a route to voice chat. We mention it because it shows the depth of the demand, and because some readers will enjoy it. For everyone else, the app update is the whole story.

It is also worth saying what the gadget is not. It does not unlock voice chat on your account, and it does not make you part of the rollout. It is a separate machine talking to Muse through tools Meta published for builders, which is precisely why it works today for readers outside the US, while the feature in the app has not reached their countries yet.

A note on accessibility

The rollout changes this note. In the US, Voice is now live for every user, including users who cannot type comfortably and for whom voice is the primary interface. That wait is over. Outside the US it is not: Meta has published no schedule for Voice in other countries, and no accessibility timeline for it either.

Before the rollout, the gap was visible in the open. In an October 13 discussion, a blind user reported accessibility bugs in the current app and said an early access request for voice, sent through Muse itself, went nowhere. That report is part of why a published schedule matters: until Meta gives one, every date attached to Voice outside the US, on any site, is a guess.
