How to Use the Evernote Connector in Muse

Connect Evernote to Muse and years of saved notes stop gathering dust and start answering questions: what did you save about this client, what did that article say, and what were you thinking last quarter. Here is how to connect it and what to expect.

1. The short answer

As of October 25, 2026, Evernote is a live connector in Muse. It was in the in app list by September 29, 2026, and Evernote confirmed it in its own release notes for version 11.37.5, published October 7, 2026, which list a new connector with Meta Muse. It was still listed in the October 15 in app check, which counted 54 connectors in the web app. There is no October change to how it works. One honest caveat belongs right here at the top: neither Meta nor Evernote has published a detailed list of what the connector can do inside Evernote, so this guide describes notes access in plain terms, tells you exactly what is confirmed and what is not, and shows you how to read the permission screen on your own account before you trust it with anything important.

Here is the honest version. The Evernote connector matters because your notes are where your thinking already lives. Articles you saved to read later, meeting notes, research for a decision, clippings from the web, ideas you wrote down at midnight and never looked at again. Before this connector, that material sat in a separate app that Muse could not see. With it, your saved notes become context Muse can search and reason from, which changes the kind of questions you can ask: not just what did I save about this, but what did I conclude, what did I miss, and what should I do with it now. The rest of this guide covers how to connect it, the setup to use on day one, and the uses that are actually worth your time, with the limits stated plainly where the published record runs out.

2. What you need

  • Muse access. You need an account with access first, in the United States, Canada, or Japan, where Muse has launched.
  • One Evernote account. The connector works with the Evernote account you connect, and only that account. Pick the one where your notes actually live.
  • Your Evernote sign in. You connect with your Evernote account, the same way you sign in to Evernote itself.
  • Approvals set to ask me. Before you connect anything, set approvals so nothing is created, changed, or shared without your tap. This is the single most important step on this page, because a notes app holds your half formed thoughts as well as your finished ones.
  • Notebooks worth searching. The connector is only as good as what is in your Evernote. Notes with clear titles and a few tags beat a pile of untitled clippings. Spend ten minutes titling the notes that matter before you connect, and everything after gets easier.

3. How to connect it, step by step

The connection itself takes a few minutes. The setup around it matters more, because neither vendor has published a full action list, so run the steps in order and read every screen before you accept it.

  1. Open Muse and go to Settings. The connectors list lives there. What is printed there is what your account has, because rollout is staged and two accounts can see different lists on the same day.
  2. Find Evernote in the connectors list and open it. If Evernote is not listed, your account has not received it yet. There is nothing to install and nothing you broke. Check again in a few days.
  3. Click Connect and review the connection details. Read what Muse is asking for before you accept it. This screen is your source of truth for what the connector can do on your account, so read it slowly. If a scope surprises you, stop and check what it covers before continuing.
  4. Sign in to Evernote and authorize Muse. Use the Evernote account where your notes live. Remember this is the one account the connector will use, so sign in with the right one the first time.
  5. Open the connector settings and check each action. After you connect, look at the per action settings in Muse. Set anything that creates, changes, or shares notes to ask you first. If an action is listed that you do not want Muse to have at all, turn it off here rather than hoping you will remember later.
  6. Set approvals to ask me. In Muse settings, set approvals so that anything that touches your notes waits for you. This is what stops Muse from ever changing a note you have not reviewed.
  7. Test with a read only request. Ask Muse to find a note you know is there and summarize it. Read the summary against the original note. If it matches, the connection is good. Do not ask it to change anything on the first day.
  8. Use it read only for a week. Finding, reading, and summarizing only. Once you trust what it reads and how it describes your notes, start approving one small change at a time.

4. If nothing matches, say so. Do not guess and do not invent a note.
5. End by asking me what to do next. Do not act until I say so.

4. What it actually does

The connector earns its place with five uses. Everything else is a variation on one of them. One note of honesty runs through all five: the exact list of actions on your account is the one shown in your connector settings, because neither vendor has published a full list. The uses below describe notes access as it works in practice, and the permission screen is the final word on your account.

Find a note by what it means, not what it is called

Ask Muse to find the note where you saved that pricing idea, the article about onboarding, or the clipping from March about a supplier. This beats searching Evernote yourself when you remember the meaning of a note but not its title, because Muse reads for meaning. Evernote search is exact and fast when you remember a word. Muse is better when you remember an idea. Use both: Muse to find the thread of your thinking, Evernote itself to check the exact words before you quote them. Our Gmail connector guide covers the same habit for your inbox, finding the message that sent the note in the first place.

Summarize a notebook before you open it

Point Muse at a notebook and ask for the shape of it: what is in there, what themes repeat, and what is missing. This is the use that pays off before a decision, because a notebook you built over months holds a view you have forgotten you had. A good notebook summary tells you what you already concluded, which saves you from researching the same question twice. It also tells you where your notes disagree with each other, which is usually where the real decision sits. Run it before a planning session, before a client call, and at the start of a quarter, when last quarter's notes are the cheapest research you own.

Pull research together for a decision

When a decision is on the table, ask Muse to gather everything your notes say about it: the articles you saved, the meeting notes where it came up, the numbers you clipped, and the idea you wrote down and forgot. Muse reads across notebooks and hands you a short brief with the strongest points on each side. The brief is a starting point, not a verdict, because your notes are a record of what you noticed, not a complete picture of the world. Check the brief against the original notes before you act on it, especially for any number or date, and go looking for what your notes never covered. A brief that only repeats your own notes is useful. A brief you treat as the whole truth is not.

Prep for a meeting from your own notes

Before a meeting, ask Muse what your notes say about the person, the project, or the topic. What did you promise last time, what did they ask for, what did you decide and then drop. This is the quiet use that makes you look prepared, because most people walk into meetings with a blank page and rely on memory. Your notes are a better memory than your memory. Pair this with the calendar side of the habit: our Google Calendar connector guide covers the day briefing, and Evernote covers what you already know about the people in it.

Review what you saved and never used

Most Evernote accounts hold a large pile of saved material nobody ever opened again. Ask Muse to review a notebook and sort it: still useful, out of date, or worth deleting. This is a decluttering use with a real payoff, because a smaller set of notes you trust beats a large set you do not. Do it once a quarter, one notebook at a time, and approve every deletion yourself. Never let Muse delete notes without your tap on each one. A note you delete in a hurry is a note you cannot get back, and the one you most want back is always the one you deleted without reading.

5. What it cannot do

The limits are honest and worth knowing before you lean on the connector.

  • It cannot tell you its full action list from a public page. Neither Meta nor Evernote has published a detailed list of what the connector can do inside Evernote. The permission screen on your own account is the source of truth. If an action matters to you, check that screen before you plan around it, and do not assume a use exists because another connector has it.
  • It cannot see notes outside the account you connected. Notes in another Evernote account, another notes app, or a local folder are invisible to it. It reasons from what you connected, nothing more. Our connectors list shows the other notes tools Muse can reach if your thinking lives in more than one place.
  • It cannot guarantee a quote is exact. A summary can smooth over a careful wording, and a paraphrase can drift from what the note actually said. Check anything you plan to repeat to someone else against the original note before you say it out loud or put it in writing.
  • It cannot fix a messy notes pile. If your notebooks are full of untitled clippings with no tags, Muse will summarize the pile. The answers get dramatically better once you title the notes that matter and tag the ones you return to. Ten minutes of titling beats ten prompts asking Muse to guess what a note called Untitled is about.
  • It cannot replace your judgment about what to keep. A note can be out of date and still worth keeping, because it shows how your thinking changed. Muse can sort by usefulness, but only you know which old note is a record worth keeping and which one is clutter.

6. What to watch for

As of October 25, 2026, Evernote is a settled connector: in the in app list by September 29, confirmed by Evernote's own release notes on October 7, and still listed in the October 15 in app check. There is no October update to how it works, so the habits around it still matter. The most important habit is the verification habit. A notes app is a personal record, and a wrong summary of your own notes feels right precisely because it sounds like you. Check the summary against the note for the first week, especially any number, date, or quote. A wrong date acted on fast is worse than a slow read you verified.

Be careful with sensitive notes. Personal journals, health notes, legal threads, and anything with another person's private information deserve a human read before any change goes out, even a small one. A note is not a document you publish. It is a thought you had, and the moment you start approving changes without reading is the moment the connector stops being an assistant and starts being a risk. Set a rule for yourself: Muse finds and drafts, you read, you approve. That rule costs seconds and saves the kind of mistake that takes weeks to undo.

Check your data controls. In Muse settings, open Data controls and look at the switch for helping improve the models. If you do not want your chats used for training, turn it off before you start asking Muse to read your notes. That switch controls whether Meta may use your chats to train future models. And remember the account split when you plan: if your work notes and your personal notes live in different Evernote accounts, choose the work one for Muse and keep the personal one to yourself. That split is also good privacy hygiene.

Finally, treat the missing public action list as a reason to check, not a reason to worry. Open the connector settings once a month and look at what is listed there. Rollouts change what an account can do, and the settings page is where the change shows up first. If a new action appears that you do not want, turn it off there and then. A connector you check monthly is a connector you control. A connector you set up once and forget is a connector that slowly drifts away from what you agreed to.

Frequently asked questions

What can Muse do with my Evernote notes?
Muse can search your notes by meaning, summarize notebooks, and pull research together for a decision. The exact list of actions on your account is shown in the connector settings, because neither Meta nor Evernote has published a full public list. Read that screen before you plan around any action.
Which Evernote account does Muse see?
Only the one you connect. Muse works with the notes available through that Evernote account and the access you authorize. Notes in another account stay invisible to it, and you can manage or disconnect the integration at any time.
How do I know the Evernote connector is real?
Evernote confirmed it in its own release notes for version 11.37.5, published October 7, 2026, which list a new connector with Meta Muse. It was in the in app list by September 29, 2026, and still listed in the October 15 in app check.
Can I use the Evernote connector outside the United States, Canada, and Japan?
Not yet. Muse itself has launched in the United States, Canada, and Japan only. Join the waitlist and we will send you a fresh code on launch day in your country.
Will Muse change or delete my notes?
Not without your approval when your approvals are set to ask me. Set anything that creates, changes, or shares notes to ask first, and approve every deletion yourself, one note at a time.
What happens if I disconnect Evernote?
Muse loses access to those notes. Your notes stay in Evernote, and nothing Muse planned is carried out after you disconnect. You can reconnect the same account or a different one later.
