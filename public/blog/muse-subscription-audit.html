2. What you need

  • Muse access. You need an account with access first, in the United States, Canada, or Japan, where Muse has launched.
  • Twelve months of bank and card statements. A single month hides annual charges. Twelve months catches the yearly plan that bills once and the free trial that converted nine months ago.
  • Your email receipts. Connect Gmail so Muse can search welcome mails, renewal notices, and receipts. Our Gmail connector guide covers the connection and the one account limit.
  • Your app store subscriptions. Apple and Google bill subscriptions that never appear as a merchant name you recognize. Open the subscriptions page on your phone and give Muse the list, or let it read the receipts in your mail.
  • Approvals set to ask me. Cancellations are money decisions, so set approvals before you start. Our permissions guide is the prerequisite for everything on this page: read it first, set ask me, then audit.
  • One hour and a willingness to cancel. The audit finds the charges. You still have to say yes to cancelling them, and the charges you keep out of sentiment are the ones that cost you next year.

3. The method, step by step

Run the audit in one sitting if you can. A half finished list is how a charge survives to next year.

  1. Pull twelve months of charges. Ask Muse to list every recurring charge in your bank and card statements for the last twelve months: merchant, amount, frequency, and last charge date. Annual charges show up here, and monthly charges show up twelve times, which is the point.
  2. Add the email receipts. Ask Muse to search your mail for welcome mails, renewal notices, and receipts, and match them against the statement list. A receipt with no matching charge is a subscription billed to a different card. A charge with no receipt is one to look at closely.
  3. Add the app store list. Open subscriptions on your phone and read Muse the list, or ask it to find the app store receipts in your mail. Phone billed subscriptions are the ones most audits miss.
  4. Sort every charge into four piles. Keep, cancel, downgrade, or pause. Keep means you used it in the last month. Cancel means you have not used it in three months. Downgrade means you use it but not the top tier. Pause means you use it seasonally, which is exactly the Brady Hanna move: cancel the sports add on in January, restore it in September.
  5. Check the annual math before you cancel. Ask Muse to annualize each monthly charge and show you the yearly total for the cancel pile. A $9 monthly charge is $108 a year, and five of them are $540. The yearly number is what makes the decision easy.
  6. Cancel one by one, with confirmation. Approve each cancellation yourself and save the confirmation page or email for every one. Some cancellations need a phone call or an in person visit, and a gym membership is the classic case: many clubs still require you to cancel at the front desk or by mail. If Muse hits a wall like that, it should tell you the exact step, not pretend the job is done.
  7. Set renewal reminders. For everything you kept, ask Muse to set a reminder a week before each renewal date, especially the annual ones. The audit is not a one time event. The reminder is what stops next year from looking like this year.
  8. Repeat every three months. A short version of the same audit each quarter catches the new trial that converted and the plan whose price went up without you noticing. Put it on the calendar now, while the savings are fresh.

5. What to watch for

Annual plans are the first trap. A monthly view hides them, which is why this guide insists on twelve months of statements. An annual plan also punishes a late cancel more than a monthly one: miss the renewal date and you pay for another full year. Check the renewal date of every annual plan you keep, and set the reminder a week early.

Cancellation friction is the second trap, and gyms are the textbook case. Many gym memberships cannot be cancelled online at all: the club requires a visit to the front desk or a mailed written notice, and some require notice at least eight days before the next billing date. Muse can find the policy and draft the letter, but it cannot walk into your club. If a cancellation needs you in person, put it on your calendar the same day you approve it, or it will not happen.

Rejoining has a price too. Cancelling a grandfathered rate can mean rejoining at a higher one, and some services charge a startup fee for a new agreement. Planet Fitness is the live example: members keep the rate they joined at while they stay members, so a three month cancel to save $30 can cost $60 more a year on rejoining, plus fees. Run the rejoining math before you cancel anything with a protected rate, and use pause or freeze where the service offers it.

Watch for the downgrade before the cancel. A streaming plan you use twice a month does not need the top tier, and a software tool you open weekly does not need the team plan. Downgrading keeps the service and cuts the bill, and it is the right answer for the services you actually use but overpay for. The audit sorts these into their own pile for a reason.

Finally, keep the confirmation for every cancellation. Save the confirmation page, the confirmation email, and the date the billing stops. Check the next statement to confirm the charge is actually gone. A cancellation that nobody confirmed is a charge that comes back, and the statement check is the only proof that counts. Should I cancel or downgrade?
