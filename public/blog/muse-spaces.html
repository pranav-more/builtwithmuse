What is Muse Spaces?

Muse Spaces is a custom screen you create inside the Muse app by describing an idea in plain language. You tell Muse what you need, and it builds the thing: a tracker, a planner, a project board, a dashboard, or a game. It is not a chat answer that scrolls away. The Space becomes part of Muse, so you can return to it any time, open it from your sidebar, and keep working in it.

Meta describes the idea in one line: describe an idea in chat, and Muse builds a space for you, whether that is a tracker, a planner or a project board. The point is that the app shapes itself around your routines, goals and interests, instead of giving you the same screen as everyone else.

A Space is also different from a workflow. Workflows are automations Muse runs for you. A Space is a place you go back to, with your stuff in it, laid out the way you asked for. You can check things off inside it, add notes, and change how it looks, and everything is still there when you return.

When did Muse Spaces launch?

November 6, 2026. Meta announced Spaces in a Newsroom post called Design the Muse App, Just for You, published at 18:00 UTC, and the feature went live the same day. TechCrunch reported it the same evening.

Spaces is the second big Muse feature in two days. Muse Voice, the talking avatar, rolled out to all users in the US the day before, on November 5. That closeness caused one mix up worth knowing about: a few posts dated Spaces to November 5. The date to use is November 6, the day Meta published the announcement.

Spaces arrived two months after the US launch of Muse on September 8, and one day after Muse launched in Mexico, its fifth country.

Where is Muse Spaces available?

Meta says Spaces is available everywhere Muse is available, and it names no countries of its own. Muse is live in five countries as of November 5, 2026: the US, Canada, Japan, Singapore and Mexico. So Spaces should be available in those five markets.

Canada is confirmed in coverage, and the announcement covers every region where Muse is offered. On devices, the picture is thinner. The iPhone app, version 14.3, released November 6, describes Spaces in its App Store release notes, which confirms iOS. Meta has not confirmed Spaces separately on Mac, on the web, or on Android, and it has not said anything about Spaces inside WhatsApp.

One caution: the announcement says starting today, but at least one outlet notes it has not confirmed Spaces actually appearing on every account yet. A staged rollout per account is possible, so if you do not see Spaces on your account, wait and check again before assuming it is missing for good.

How do you create a Space?

Open Muse and describe the idea in chat. That is the whole instruction Meta gives: tell Muse about it, and it builds it for you. There is no published step by step from Meta, and no official help page for Spaces yet.

In practice, creating a Space looks like this, based on the announcement and a user demo:

  1. Open Muse and start a chat.
  2. Describe what you want, in plain language. Name the job it should do, not the layout. A gift tracker for the holidays works better than a three column board.
  3. Muse may offer a suggested shape first, then build it once you confirm. In one demo, a user asked for a space about testing, Muse offered a simple playground to try things, and built The Testing Playground, a full screen page with nine interactive widgets: a counter, a reaction test, a color mixer, a type tester, a sound board, a chance machine, a doodle pad, a stopwatch and a scratchpad.
  4. The Space appears in the Muse app, in the sidebar, under a Create a space entry.

The more specific your description, the better the result. Say who it is for, what goes in it, and what you want to do with it. A wedding planner with an RSVP list, vendor invoices and a to do list gives Muse more to build than a wedding thing.

What can you build with Spaces?

Meta lists nine examples in the announcement, and they cover the everyday jobs people already ask Muse about:

  • A shopping hub. Holiday and birthday gift ideas, wishlists from Instagram saves, Marketplace listings and buyer messages in one view.
  • A school dashboard. It finds the events relevant to your kids in school emails and adds them to your calendar.
  • A music tracker. Artists you love, with new releases and upcoming shows.
  • A wedding planner. RSVP list, vendor invoices, and to dos.
  • A trivia game. Quizzes on your favorite movies, music, or sports team.
  • A podcast studio. Episode ideas, research, and scripts together.
  • An order tracker for a small business. New orders, customer messages, and what needs to ship today.
  • A client hub for freelance work. Projects, deadlines, and invoices.
  • A trip planner. Flights, hotel, reservations, and a day by day itinerary.

Mark Zuckerberg posted his own three on X the same day: a BBQ game, an event tracker, and a home control dashboard.

The official demo video shows three more: a Family Dashboard with school schedules, pickups, calendar items and a lunchbox planner; Jolly Mall, a retro style shopping catalog built from Instagram saves with buy now functionality; and a Scotland Trip Planner that adds a booked golf tee time.

The pattern is clear. If the job repeats, and it has a list, a date, or a collection attached to it, it fits a Space. If it is a one off question, a chat answer is enough.

How do you change a Space after you make it?

Your first version is a starting point, not a finished product. Meta says you can keep adjusting a Space with Muse as you use it, whether that is adding what is missing, trying a new look, or changing how it works.

You can also edit directly inside the Space. Check things off, add notes, and make edits right there, and everything will be there when you return. The Space remembers its state between visits, so a tracker you update on Monday is still updated on Friday.

If the layout stops fitting, tell Muse what to change in chat. Add a section for gift ideas under 50 dollars, or move the calendar to the top, are the kind of instructions Spaces is built for. You do not need to rebuild from scratch, and you do not need to describe the whole Space again.

How do Spaces stay up to date?

Connect the apps you use, and your Spaces keep themselves up to date in the background. Meta gives three examples: new concert dates, price changes, and savings progress show up without you having to ask.

The connected apps named in coverage are Gmail, Google Calendar, OpenTable and Notion. That list comes from reporting on the launch, not from an official Spaces support list from Meta, so treat it as the apps known to work with Muse, not a promise that every connector feeds every Space.

What Meta has not disclosed is how often the background updates happen, and how accurate they are. A Space that pulls concert dates from your calendar is only as fresh as the last sync, and no source gives a refresh schedule. If a date matters, check the source app before you act on it.

This is the part of Spaces that makes it more than a static page. A trip planner that adds a booked tee time by itself, or a school dashboard that finds events in your email, is doing work while you are not looking. That is also why the connected apps matter: a Space with nothing connected is a page you update by hand.

How do you organize your Spaces?

Spaces live where you put them. You can add them to your sidebar, move them into your main tabs, hide the ones you do not need right now, and schedule when they appear. Meta gives one example: keep your weekly planner up front during the workweek, then swap it for your weekend plans on Saturday.

The demo shows drag to reorder tabs, and a spoken command that reorganizes the sidebar: typing Weekend mode please swaps the layout to prioritize weekend Spaces. That is the idea in one line. The app rearranges itself around what you are doing, instead of you rearranging the app.

Start with two or three Spaces, not ten. A sidebar full of half built trackers is worse than a chat history. Keep the ones you open every day up front, hide the seasonal ones, and delete the experiments that did not work. Spaces are cheap to make, so the cost is not creation, it is clutter.

Can you share a Space with someone else?

No, not at launch. Your Spaces are personal from the start. Meta says it is exploring ways for you to share your Spaces with others to build a more collaborative experience in Muse, but it gives no date, no method, and no promise.

That means there is no shared editing, no public publishing, and no link you can send someone to open your Space. A wedding planner you build is yours alone until Meta ships sharing. If you need two people in the same Space today, a shared doc or a shared Muse chat is still the way to do it.

Sharing is the obvious next step, and the announcement frames it that way. A trip planner, a school dashboard, or a client hub all get better when more than one person can see them. For now, build for yourself, and treat sharing as coming later.

Does Muse Spaces cost extra?

Meta announced no price, no plan, and no usage limit for Spaces. There is no Spaces fee in any source checked, and no waitlist to join.

Muse itself is free to use, with a weekly usage limit, and two paid plans that buy more usage: Power at $20 a month, and Maximum at $100 a month. Meta describes a subscription as additional usage, not additional features. That makes it likely Spaces is available to free users inside their weekly allowance, but that is an inference from how Muse pricing works, not a confirmed fact. Do not read this guide as a promise that Spaces is free forever, and check the Muse pricing page if the cost matters to you.

No source states a cap on how many Spaces one user can create, or any Spaces specific quota. If you build a lot of them, the limit you will hit first is your weekly Muse usage, not a Spaces count.

What can Spaces not do yet?

A fair reading of the launch, and of what Meta did not say:

  • You cannot share a Space. Personal only, for now.
  • You cannot publish a Space as a standalone app. Spaces live inside Muse, and the announcement describes no way to turn one into a public link or a separate app.
  • There is no stated cap on how many Spaces you can make, and no stated usage limit, which means there is also no promise. The limits, if any, are undisclosed.
  • There is no official help page for Spaces yet. The Newsroom post is the only primary documentation so far.
  • Nothing in the announcement says what happens to a Space if you disconnect an app, or whether Spaces work offline. Do not assume either way.
  • The creation flow has no published step by step. The sidebar entry and the chat prompt are what the demos show, and Meta has not documented the exact UI.

None of this is a reason to skip Spaces. It is a reason to build your first one on something low stakes, like a gift tracker, and see how it behaves before you put a business workflow in it.

How is Spaces different from Muse Voice?

Spaces and Voice launched one day apart, and they solve different problems. Voice, which rolled out to all users in the US on November 5, 2026, is about talking to your Muse agent with a real time voice and an expressive avatar. Spaces, launched November 6, is about building a custom screen you return to.

Voice changes how you talk to Muse. Spaces changes what Muse shows you. You can use both: talk to Muse about a trip, and keep the trip planner Space it built.

The same announcement also says Meta is rolling out specialized versions of its models to Muse, trained with learnings from Muse Wonder, named in coverage as Active Memory and Active Focus. Those are model upgrades, not part of Spaces, and they deserve their own read. Do not confuse a smarter Muse with a Space. A Space is a screen. The model is the brain behind it.

Three Spaces to try first

Start with one of these. Each one uses a real job, and each prompt gives Muse enough detail to build something useful.

  1. A holiday gift tracker. Say: Build me a gift tracker for the holidays. I need a list for each person, gift ideas with prices, what I have bought, and what still needs wrapping. Put the people I have not bought for yet at the top. Connect it to your Instagram saves if you want wishlists to flow in.
  2. A trip planner. Say: Build me a trip planner for a weekend in Goa. I need flights, a hotel, restaurant reservations, and a day by day plan. Add a section for things I still need to book. Then book something, and watch whether the Space picks it up.
  3. A client hub. Say: Build me a client hub for my freelance work. I need one card per client with the project, the deadline, the invoice amount, and whether it is paid. Sort by deadline, and show what is overdue first.

Build one, use it for a week, and change it twice. That tells you more about Spaces than any guide can.
