What did Loblaw and Meta announce?

On November 18, 2026, Loblaw Companies Limited announced it is making its product catalogue available through Muse, Meta's personal AI agent, and Meta confirmed the same day that Loblaw is the first Canadian retailer to do so. The announcement came in two posts published hours apart: Loblaw's own release at 8:00 a.m. Eastern time and Meta's newsroom post at 14:07 UTC. Both say the same thing in the same words. Canadians can now go from store selection to checkout at participating Loblaw banners using Muse, without leaving the app. The Loblaw release is at the GlobeNewswire announcement, and Meta's version is at the Meta newsroom post.

The timing matters because Muse itself has only been in Canada since September 18, 2026, two months to the day before this launch. Canada was the second country in the world to get Muse, after the US, and Loblaw is the first Canadian retailer to build on it. Meta's post frames the launch as part of a wider pattern: Canadians can already connect Muse to Gmail, Google Calendar and Instagram, and Loblaw is the first homegrown shopping connector on that list. The launch also landed on the same day Meta rolled out Muse Voice in Canada, so November 18, 2026 was the biggest single day for Muse in Canada since the launch itself. Voice is covered in the Muse Voice in Canada guide.

Loblaw calls the move part of its push into what the industry calls agentic commerce, shopping where an agent does the finding, the choosing and the buying on your behalf. That phrase is worth translating into plain terms. Before this launch, Muse could talk about groceries and write you a list. Now it can act on the list: find the products in Loblaw's catalogue, put them in a basket, and complete the purchase. The agent is no longer a research assistant for your shop. It is the shop, with you approving each step. How that approval works is covered below, because it is the part that decides whether this feels useful or unnerving.

How does shopping Loblaw with Muse work?

Start with Muse itself. You need a Muse account in Canada, on iPhone, Android, the web at muse.ai, or the Mac app, and the Loblaw connector connected inside it. Connectors are the apps and services Muse can act through, and Loblaw now sits on that list for Canadians alongside Gmail, Google Calendar and Instagram. The exact taps to connect Loblaw live inside your Muse account, under the connectors list, and they are the first thing to check if the option does not appear: connectors roll out to accounts over days, so an account that cannot see Loblaw today may see it tomorrow. If you are new to Muse in Canada, the Canada guide covers setup on every device.

Once Loblaw is connected, the flow is a conversation. Tell Muse what you need, in your own words. Plan dinners for the week for two people, one of them vegetarian. Restock the usuals. Find the ingredients for a specific recipe. Muse works from Loblaw's catalogue, so it suggests real products at real Loblaw prices rather than inventing items, and it builds the basket as you talk. You can change quantities, swap a brand, or remove an item at any point, the same way you would edit a basket on a grocery site. The difference is that the basket lives in the chat, and the chat is where you change it.

Checkout is the step where Muse asks first. Meta's post is explicit on this point: Muse checks with you before sensitive actions such as making a purchase. Nothing is charged because Muse decided it was time. You review the basket, confirm the store and the total, and approve the purchase. That approval is the whole safety model for agentic shopping, and it is worth treating as a habit rather than a formality. Read the basket before you approve it, the same way you would read a receipt before you pay. The first shop is the one to do slowly, with a small basket, so you learn what the approval screen shows before you trust it with a full weekly shop.

One practical note before your first order: the announcement does not say whether the order is delivered, picked up in store, or both, and it does not say which provinces are covered on day one. That is not a gap in this guide. It is a gap in the announcement, and it is covered in the section on delivery below. The honest instruction is to start the flow in Muse and read what it offers you for your address before you plan around a delivery slot that may not exist yet.

What does Muse remember about your shopping?

The feature that makes the second shop easier than the first is memory. Meta says Muse can remember brand preferences and information like food allergies, making next week's planning even easier. In practice that means the agent carries what it learns about your household from one shop to the next. If you always buy the same oat milk, it can default to it. If someone in the house has a nut allergy, it can steer meal plans away from nuts without being told again. The value is not in any single remembered fact. It is in the planning getting shorter every week because the agent stops asking questions it already knows the answers to.

That memory is also the part to think about before you hand over your shopping. A grocery list is a surprisingly personal record: what you eat, what you avoid, what you buy for children, what you buy when you are ill. Muse stores the data and credentials for the services you connect on Muse Secure VM, a dedicated computer in the cloud, and Meta says passwords and payment methods stay out of the agent's view. You choose what your Muse can access. The control that matters most is the data controls setting, where you can turn off the option labelled "Help improve our AI models" so your activity is not used for model training. That setting is covered in the Canada guide, and it is worth setting before your first shop rather than after your tenth.

A sensible rule for the first month is to treat Muse's memory as a draft, not a record. Check the basket every time, even when the agent says it remembered your usuals. Preferences drift: a brand you liked last month may be one you have dropped, and an allergy note from one conversation may not cover a new restriction. The agent gets better with correction, and correcting it is fast. Saying "not that brand any more" in the chat is the whole fix. The shoppers who will get the most from this connector are the ones who correct it early and often, because every correction is what the next plan is built from.

Which Loblaw banners work with Muse?

This is the question the announcement does not answer, and it is the most important gap in the launch. Both Meta and Loblaw say only "participating Loblaw banners." Neither names a single one. That phrase covers a large family of stores: Loblaws, No Frills, Real Canadian Superstore, Fortinos, Zehrs and Shoppers Drug Mart are all Loblaw banners, and they serve very different shoppers at very different prices. A No Frills shopper and a Loblaws shopper are not buying the same basket, so which banners take part decides who this launch is actually for. Until Meta or Loblaw publishes a list, no guide can tell you that your store is in. Any guide that names banners today is guessing.

The practical way to find out is inside Muse. Connect the Loblaw connector and ask it which store it can shop at for your address. The connector can only offer stores that are actually participating, so the answer you get in the app is the authority, not any article. If your usual store is not offered, try a second nearby banner before concluding the launch has passed you by. Rollouts like this one often start with a subset of banners and add more over weeks, so a store that is missing in November may appear in December. Check again rather than writing the launch off.

There is a second reason the banner list matters: price. Loblaw's banners do not share one price list, and a basket built from a Loblaws catalogue will not cost the same as the same basket from No Frills. When you review the basket before approving checkout, check the store name on the basket as well as the total. If Muse planned your meals against one banner's prices and checked out at another, the total will surprise you. The store name is on the approval screen for exactly this reason, and reading it is the cheapest habit in this whole guide.

What about delivery, pickup, fees and PC Optimum?

Short answer: Meta and Loblaw have not said. The announcements describe meal planning, basket building and checkout, and they say nothing about whether the order arrives at your door, waits for pickup in store, or either. They say nothing about delivery fees, minimum order sizes, or which provinces are covered. Independent coverage the same day flagged exactly these gaps: the announcement does not identify the participating banners or spell out delivery coverage and fees. That is the honest state of the public record on November 19, 2026, and this guide will not fill it with guesses.

The same silence covers PC Optimum, Loblaw's loyalty program. Loblaw has not said whether Optimum points are earned on Muse orders, whether offers apply, or whether your Optimum account connects to Muse at all. For many Canadian shoppers that is not a small detail. Optimum points are the reason a large share of Loblaw customers choose one banner over another, and an order that earns no points is an order that costs more in real terms than the same order placed in the PC Express app. Until Loblaw says otherwise, assume the loyalty side is not connected, and weigh that against the convenience of shopping in chat.

What to do about all of this is simple: ask Muse before you buy. The connector is the only source that knows what it can actually do for your address today. Ask it how the order reaches you, what the fees are, and whether your Optimum account is linked. If it cannot answer, that is itself an answer: the feature is not ready for that part of your shop yet. Check back in a few weeks, because the gaps in a launch week announcement are usually the first things the next update fills in, and this guide will be updated when Loblaw publishes the details.

Is it safe to let Muse check out for you?

The safety case Meta makes for Muse has three parts, and all three apply to shopping. First, Muse runs on Muse Secure VM, a dedicated computer in the cloud that stores the data and credentials for the services you connect, while keeping passwords and payment methods out of the agent's view. The agent can use a connected service without seeing the password that opens it. Second, you choose what your Muse can access, and you can disconnect a connector at any time. Third, Muse checks with you before sensitive actions such as sending an email or making a purchase. The purchase never happens on the agent's initiative alone.

Those three parts are reassuring, and they are also incomplete in one respect that matters for shopping: the approval screen is only as good as the attention you give it. An agent that builds a basket from a conversation can mishear a quantity, swap a product for a similar one, or add an item you mentioned as an idea rather than an order. None of that is a security failure. It is a conversation failure, and it is caught the same way every time: by reading the basket before you approve it. The approval step is the safety feature. Skipping it turns the agent from a careful shopper into a fast one, and fast is not what you want from someone spending your money.

Two habits cover the rest. First, start with a small first order, and check the charge against the basket total when it lands. If the numbers match, the flow works for your store and your address. If they do not, you have learned that on a small order, not a weekly shop. Second, keep the Loblaw connector's access narrow: connect it only if you plan to use it, and disconnect it if you stop. A connector you never use is still a door you left open. Neither habit takes more than a minute, and together they are the whole security practice for agentic grocery shopping.

Why this launch matters for grocery shopping in Canada

Loblaw is the first Canadian retailer on Muse, and first matters here because it sets the shape of what comes next. Every other Canadian retailer watching this launch is watching the same three questions: do shoppers trust an agent with their basket, does the approval step hold up under real weekly shopping, and does the memory of brands and allergies make the second shop fast enough to keep people coming back. If the answers are yes, expect more Canadian retailers to follow. If the answers are no, this stays a single retailer experiment. The launch is a test of agentic grocery shopping in Canada, and Loblaw is the retailer running it.

The launch also changes what Muse is for in Canada. Until November 18, Muse in Canada was a general assistant: email, calendar, chat, and the other connectors that shipped at launch. Shopping was something Muse could talk about but not do, except through the retailers Meta announced in September, none of them Canadian. Loblaw makes Muse a shopping agent for the first time in the country where most Canadians actually buy groceries. That is a bigger shift than a single connector. It is the moment Muse stops being a clever chat app and starts being the place a weekly shop happens.

The comparison worth watching is Australia, where Muse launched on November 17, 2026 with local shopping partners rolling out progressively. Canada got its first retail partner one day later, on November 18. The two launches are Meta running the same playbook in two countries at once: launch the agent, then add the retailers people already shop at. The countries page tracks every launch in order, and the pattern is now clear enough to predict. The next Canadian retailers will be the ones whose customers ask for them, so if you want your store on Muse, asking for it in the app is the most useful thing you can do.
