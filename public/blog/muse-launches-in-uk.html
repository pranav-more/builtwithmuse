What did Meta announce for the UK?

Meta announced Muse in the UK on November 24, 2026, in a post on the Meta Newsroom titled "Introducing Muse in the UK: A Personal AI Agent You Control". Mark Zuckerberg posted about the launch on X the same morning at 06:00 UTC. The UK becomes the seventh market where Muse is available and the first in Europe, after the United States, Canada, Japan, Singapore, Mexico and Australia. The announcement is at Meta Newsroom on Muse in the UK.

This is a full launch, not a preview or a waitlist. Muse is available to anyone aged 18 and over in the UK from November 24, 2026, on iOS, Android, muse.ai and WhatsApp. Meta says Muse is free to use for most of what people need, with subscription plans for those who want to do even more. Meta published no UK subscription prices, so what the paid plans will cost in pounds is still unknown, and this guide will be updated when Meta says more.

Muse Voice and Muse Spaces arrive with the launch too. Muse Voice is live voice conversation with hundreds of voices, including a range of British accents, so you can talk to Muse the way you would talk to a person. Muse Spaces lets you describe an idea and have Muse build a persistent mini app from it, which stays live so you can return to it and keep working on it. Both were part of the launch announcement, and both work in the UK from day one.

The timing fits the season. The launch landed in the week before Black Friday, and Meta's own examples lean into the jobs people do at this time of year: Christmas shopping, searching for a cheaper energy deal, sorting the school calendar, and turning saved travel Reels into a holiday itinerary. The UK is also the market where Muse arrives with the deepest bench of partners at launch, which is the subject of the section below.

When did Muse launch in the UK, and which countries came first?

Muse launched in the UK on November 24, 2026. The UK is the seventh market in the rollout, and the first in Europe. The order so far is:

  3. Japan, October 23, 2026
  4. Singapore, October 27, 2026
  5. Mexico, November 5, 2026
  6. Australia, November 17, 2026
  7. United Kingdom, November 24, 2026

The UK launch was expected before Meta confirmed it. The Times reported on November 8, 2026 that a UK launch was coming within weeks, and Meta declined to comment at the time. The report is at The Times on the UK launch. The same report says the Information Commissioner's Office told Meta before launch that Muse must comply with UK data protection law. Sixteen days after that report, the launch was official.

The pace of the rollout is the story behind the date. Muse went from one market to seven in under three months, with three launches in November alone: Mexico on November 5, Australia on November 17, and the UK on November 24. Each new market has arrived with its own set of local partners, and the UK set is the largest yet. That pace suggests Meta is treating the launch as a race to cover the English speaking world first, with Europe opening through London.

For people in the UK who have been watching Muse from the sidelines since September, the wait is over. There is no queue to join and no code needed to start. If you are 18 or over and in the UK, you can open Muse today in the app, in a browser, or in WhatsApp, and the same account works across all of them.

Muse in the UK works on four surfaces from launch day. There is the Muse app on iOS and the Muse app on Android, both listed as "Muse from Meta" in the app stores. There is muse.ai in a browser, which needs no install. And there is WhatsApp, where Muse works inside the app many people in the UK already open every day. One Meta account signs you in across all four, and your conversations carry over between them.

WhatsApp is the surface that matters most for everyday use in the UK. You do not need to learn a new app or keep another icon on your home screen. You message Muse the way you message a friend, and it can work on tasks in the background while you get on with your day. The WhatsApp route also puts Muse where group chats, family messages and shared plans already live, which is where a personal agent is most useful.

That means the glasses launch follows the phone, browser and WhatsApp launch rather than arriving with it. If you already own a pair, the Muse features will arrive as an update rather than on the day you first open the app.

One more surface arrived the same day, with a catch. Muse also arrived in Instagram DMs on November 24, 2026, though the UK is not in the first rollout list for that feature, so people in the UK cannot use it yet. The details are in Muse in Instagram DMs.

What the partners actually do falls into two groups. With some partners you can connect your personal account, so Muse can reorder your usual shop or pull up a booking you already made. With others, Muse can find its way around their websites from day one, searching and comparing without a connection. The split matters for what you can ask on the first day: a connected account lets Muse act on your history, while a website connection lets Muse shop and search as a guest.

The gaps are worth naming too. No UK bank is among the launch partners, so Muse cannot yet reach into your banking in the UK the way it can reach into a shop or a travel booking. PayPal is listed as coming soon rather than live, which means the payment step for now runs through the checkout systems Meta named, covered in the next section. Expect the partner list to grow, and expect banks to be the next frontier once the retail launch settles.

How does checkout work with Muse in the UK?

Checkout in Muse runs through two systems Meta named in the launch. The first is Onelink, built by Stripe, which generates a unique digital version of your card only accessible to agents using Onelink. The second is Shop Pay from Shopify, which uses your saved payment and shipping details. Both keep the actual card details away from the agent, so Muse can complete a purchase without ever seeing the numbers behind it.

The idea is that Muse pays on your behalf rather than as you. Onelink creates a card that only an agent using Onelink can use, tied to the purchase you approved. Shop Pay works the other way round: it uses the payment and shipping details you already saved, so a shop that runs on Shopify can check out in a couple of steps. Either way, the payment step happens inside the conversation, and you do not get sent off to a separate checkout page to finish the job.

Approval is the step that makes this safe. You approve important actions before Muse carries them out, and a purchase is an important action. That means Muse can find the item, compare the price, and build the basket, but the money does not move until you say yes. The habit that matters most is the same one that matters with any agent that spends: read the basket before you approve it, check the quantity and the total, and only then let the purchase go through.

What Muse cannot see is as important as what it can do. Muse has no visibility into passwords or payment methods. That is a design rule Meta states for the product, not a setting you have to turn on. Your card details stay with the payment system, and Muse works with the token or the saved details that the system gives it. If you ever want to stop a connection, you can disconnect it, and the agent loses its way in.

What can Muse do for you in the UK?

Meta's launch examples are deliberately ordinary, because ordinary is where a personal agent earns its keep. Christmas shopping is the first: ask Muse to find a gift within a budget, compare prices across the launch partners, and buy it once you approve. The weekly shop is the second: with a connected account, Muse can reorder your usual shop from Sainsbury's or Ocado Retail, or find the same items cheaper elsewhere.

Money is the third example, and the most useful. Ask Muse to search for a cheaper energy deal and it can compare tariffs, work out what you would save, and lay out the switch in plain terms. The same pattern works for travel: ask Muse to sort a trip, and it can check Trainline for train times, Jet2 or easyJet for flights, and JustPark for parking near the airport, then pull it together into one plan.

The school calendar is the fourth example, and it shows the personal side of the product. Ask Muse to sort the school calendar and it can read the dates, spot the clashes, and remind you about the events that need a reply. Turn saved travel Reels into a holiday itinerary is the fifth, and it shows the creative side: give Muse the clips you saved, and it turns them into a plan with places, days, and bookings to check.

Behind all five examples is the same shape: you describe the outcome in your own words, Muse does the searching and the comparing, and you approve the result. The launch partners are what make the examples real rather than theoretical. Without Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury's, Trainline and Jet2 connected, the Christmas shop and the holiday plan would be a chat. With them connected, they are jobs Muse can finish.

What control do you have over Muse, and how is your privacy handled?

Control is the idea Meta puts at the front of the UK launch, and the Newsroom post is titled around it: a personal AI agent you control. You choose what Muse can access, you approve important actions before Muse carries them out, and you can ask it to remember things or forget them. The forget part matters as much as the remember part, because an agent that keeps everything you tell it is only useful if you can take things back.

The privacy design has three parts, all from Meta. Every Muse gets its own virtual computer in the cloud, which is where the data and credentials for the services you connect live. Conversations and data in the virtual machine are not shared with Meta advertising systems. And you can switch off training use in Settings, so your conversations are not used to train Meta's models. Those three facts are the whole privacy case in one paragraph, and they are worth checking against the Settings screen when you first sign in.

The regulator was involved before launch. The Times reported on November 8, 2026 that the Information Commissioner's Office told Meta before launch that Muse must comply with UK data protection law. That is not a surprise: any product that handles personal data in the UK has to meet the same rules, and the regulator saying so in advance is the normal course of events. It does mean the UK launch arrives with the regulator watching, which is a good thing for people who will hand Muse their shopping, travel, and calendar.

The practical advice is simple. When you first connect a service, check what you connected and what it can see. When Muse asks for approval, read what it is asking to do before you say yes. And when you tell Muse something you would rather it did not keep, ask it to forget it, then check that it did. The controls are there, but they only work if you use them.

Partnering with beloved British brands and retailers means Muse works seamlessly with services people here use every day, making everything from the weekly shop to sorting out their travel needs easier." The quote is from the Meta Newsroom post.

The quote is worth reading twice, because it names the two bets Meta is making in the UK. The first bet is control: the words "that they control" are doing the heavy lifting, and they answer the question every new Muse user asks first, which is who is in charge. The second bet is local: "beloved British brands and retailers" is the reason the partner list leads with Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury's, Debenhams and JD Sports rather than with global names. Meta is saying the UK launch is a British product, not an American product that happens to work in Britain.

Why does the UK launch matter?

The UK is the first market in Europe, and Europe is where the rules are strictest. Launching in London first, with the Information Commissioner's Office already engaged, sets the pattern for how Muse will enter the rest of Europe. If the UK launch works, with the privacy controls and the approval steps holding up under real use, the next European markets follow the same template. If it stumbles, the delay will be measured in regulation as well as in engineering.

The partner list is the second reason it matters. Fifteen UK brands and retailers at launch is the deepest retail bench Muse has opened with in any market, and it covers the four kinds of spending that define a British week: the food shop, the high street, travel, and beauty. That makes the UK the first market where Muse can plausibly do the whole week, from the Sainsbury's order to the Trainline ticket to the Jet2 flight, without leaving the conversation.

The timing is the third reason. Launching in the week before Black Friday, with Christmas shopping as the headline example, puts Muse in front of people at the exact moment they are spending the most and comparing the hardest. A personal agent that can find a cheaper energy deal in November and sort the Christmas shop in December is not a demo. It is a product with a season to prove itself in, and the UK is the market where it has to prove it.
