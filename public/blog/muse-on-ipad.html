How to Use Muse on iPad

Muse has a native iPad app, shipped October 7, 2026, with version 10.0. It is free on the App Store, needs iPadOS 18.0 or later, and is built for the bigger screen with iPadOS multitasking. It is the same agent as on iPhone, with more room to read, check, and approve its work.

Is there a Muse app for iPad?

Yes. Muse got a native iPad app on October 7, 2026, about a month after the iPhone and Android launch on September 8. The iPad support arrived inside version 10.0 of the Muse app, and the App Store release notes said it in one line: "Muse is now available on iPad." TechCrunch reported the release the same day, and The Verge covered it as a native tablet build rather than a scaled up phone app.

Native matters here. Before October 7, the only way to run Muse on an iPad was the iPhone app stretched to fit, which wastes the screen and handles multitasking badly. The native app is built for the bigger display, with a layout that uses the width and with support for iPadOS multitasking, so you can keep Muse open next to your inbox, a document, or a spreadsheet. The agent is the same. The room it has to work in is what changed.

The iPad app is free on the App Store, like the iPhone app. Paid plans, Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100 a month, buy weekly usage capacity, not extra features, and the same plans cover the iPad. There is no separate iPad subscription, no iPad only price, and no extra fee for the bigger screen. If you already pay for Muse on your phone, the iPad uses the same plan and the same account.

The timing fits a fast rollout. Muse launched on iPhone, Android, and the web on September 8, reached the Mac about a week later, and got the iPad on October 7. The watch followed a month after that, on November 9. The iPad was the first big screen version of Muse, and it set the pattern: the same agent, reshaped for the device it runs on.

How do you install Muse on iPad?

Open the App Store on your iPad and search for "Muse from Meta." That is the exact listing name, and it matters, because the Meta AI app is a different app and is not the iPad version of Muse. Install the listing named Muse from Meta. The listing covers iPhone and iPad together, and it asks for iPadOS 18.0 or later.

Check your iPadOS version before you install. If your iPad runs an older version, the App Store will not offer the app, or will offer an older version without iPad support. Update to iPadOS 18 in Settings, under General and Software Update, then try again. Which iPads can run iPadOS 18 depends on the model, and Apple publishes that list. If your iPad cannot update, it cannot run the native Muse app.

Sign in with your Meta account, the same one you use on iPhone if you already use Muse there. You are not creating a second Muse. The account, the connected apps, the chat history, and the usage allowance all carry over, because they belong to the account, not to the device. If you are new, you will create a Meta account during setup, and Muse is for people 18 and over.

If the app does not appear in search results at all, check your App Store region first. Muse's rollout is limited by country, and the iPad app follows the same country list as the rest of Muse. No reinstall will override a region limit. The country section below covers exactly where the iPad app is available and what to do if your region is not on the list.

How do you set it up?

Setup on iPad follows the same path as on iPhone, because it is the same app. Sign in, grant the permissions Muse asks for, and connect the apps the task needs. Connect only the apps a task actually needs, rather than connecting everything at once. Each connection is a door into an account, and a tidy set of connections is easier to audit than a drawer full of them.

Keep approvals set to ask before Muse sends, books, or buys anything. That is the setting that keeps an agent useful instead of risky, and the iPad's bigger screen makes the approval step easier, not harder. A longer draft is easier to skim on an iPad, which is a reason to read it, not a reason to tap through it. Read the approval, check the details, then allow or deny.

Start with one small read only task. Ask Muse to summarize something, research something, or draft something, and read the result against the source beside it. That first task teaches you how Muse behaves on the iPad without risking a booking or a purchase. Once you trust the pattern, move to tasks that act: bookings, messages, and orders, each with approval on.

Keep tasks in separate side chats, named for the job. One side chat for a trip, one for a school week, one for a client. The menu sits in the top left, and the new chat control is the pencil and note icon. Side chats keep the main thread from becoming one endless scroll, and on a big screen they are easy to switch between. The Library tab holds the files Muse generates: documents, spreadsheets, images, and podcasts, saved where you can find them again.

What is different from Muse on iPhone?

The layout. The iPad app puts saved chats in a sidebar and gives the conversation the main canvas, according to iClarified's look at the release. On iPhone the same content stacks vertically and you scroll through it. On iPad you see where you are and what else you have open, which changes how you work with long threads. A conversation that feels endless on a phone feels navigable on a tablet.

The powers are the same. The iPad does not add a different set of abilities, and it does not skip the free usage limit. The same agent, the same connectors, the same approvals, the same weekly allowance. If a task works on iPhone, it works on iPad. If it does not work on iPhone, the iPad will not fix it. What changes is the reading, the checking, and the comparing, all of which benefit from width.

The real iPad advantage is checking. A bigger screen makes it easier to read drafts, compare results, and catch mistakes before approving an action. Ask Muse for a table instead of a paragraph, and the table is easier to read on an iPad than on a phone. Ask for a file when the answer should be something you can keep, and the file opens beside the chat instead of replacing it. The iPad turns Muse from a chat you scroll into a workspace you inspect.

One difference is what is missing. No reporting describes tablet exclusive agent functions beyond the interface and multitasking advantages. There is no iPad only connector, no iPad only mode, and no iPad only feature in the release notes. The update's new connectors and business features arrived in the same version, but they are account features, not iPad features, and they work on iPhone too. Do not buy an iPad for a Muse feature that only exists on iPad, because there is not one.

How does multitasking work?

This is the step the iPad exists for. Open Muse alongside the app you are working from: your email, your calendar, a document, or a web page. Use Split View or Slide Over on most iPads, or Stage Manager where your model supports it. These are iPadOS features, not Muse features, and which ones your iPad offers depends on the model. The point is the layout, not the gesture.

The pattern is simple. Ask Muse to draft, summarize, or research something, then read its answer against the source beside it instead of taking it on trust. A summary of an email thread is more useful when the thread is open next to it. A travel plan is easier to check when the calendar is visible. A budget is easier to verify when the spreadsheet sits beside the chat. Multitasking turns checking from a chore into a glance.

Split View puts two apps side by side, each getting part of the screen. Slide Over floats a narrow app window above the full screen app, which suits a quick Muse question while you work in something else. Stage Manager, on models that support it, lets you arrange overlapping windows and switch between sets of them. Any of the three works. Pick the one your iPad supports and your hands remember.

There is one habit worth building. Put Muse on one side and the thing you are checking on the other, and keep them there for the whole task. Do not flip back and forth between full screen apps, because flipping hides the source at the exact moment you need to compare. The iPad's advantage disappears if you use it like a big phone. Use the width on purpose.

Which connectors arrived with the iPad update?

Version 10.0 added a wider connector list aimed partly at small businesses. TechCrunch named Asana, Canva, Dropbox, Figma, QuickBooks, GitHub, Klaviyo, and Zoom, along with Meta advertising accounts, plus Notion and Granola for everyday tasks. That list comes from reporting on the update, and it is the set of connections the release notes described. The full Muse connectors list has grown since, and the iPad uses the same connectors as every other Muse surface.

The connectors matter more on iPad because the screen rewards them. A Canva design, a Figma file, or a QuickBooks report is easier to review on a tablet than on a phone, and the connector is what lets Muse pull that content into the chat. Connect the apps the task needs, use them, and disconnect anything you no longer use. The connector list in Settings is the same on iPad as on iPhone.

The same update added a business goal mode. Users can set a business objective for Muse, and the agent will plan work such as marketing content, client pitches, and product pages, subject to user approval. That mode arrived in the same version as the iPad app, and reporting ties the two together as one business push. It is not iPad only, but the iPad is a natural place to review the plans it produces, because a marketing plan or a pitch draft reads better on a big screen.

If you run a small business, the combination to try is the goal mode plus two or three connectors: one for files, one for money, one for customers. Set the goal, let Muse draft the plan, and review it on the iPad with the source apps open beside it. Approve each step as it comes. The iPad does not make the agent smarter, but it makes your review of the agent's work faster and more reliable.

What is the iPad good for with Muse?

Long documents. A summary that runs to two pages, a research brief, a contract draft, or a study guide all read better on an iPad than on a phone. Ask Muse for the document, open it in the Library tab, and read it at a size where skimming works. If the document matters, read it against the source Muse used, with both open at once.

Comparisons. Flight options, hotel prices, insurance quotes, or vendor bids all come back as lists, and lists are easier to compare when you can see the whole table. Ask for a table, not a paragraph, and use the width. A table that wraps and scrolls on a phone sits still on an iPad, and still tables get compared properly.

Planning. Trip plans, event plans, and project plans all have dates, lists, and moving parts. Build the plan in a side chat named for the job, keep the calendar open beside it, and check each date as Muse proposes it. The iPad layout, with the sidebar and the canvas, suits a plan you return to over days, because you can find the thread again without scrolling through everything else.

Reviewing before approving. This is the highest value use, and the least glamorous. Muse asks for approval before sensitive actions, and the iPad shows you the full details of what you are approving: the recipient, the amount, the date, the wording. Read it all. The bigger screen removes the excuse for tapping through, and tapping through is how agent mistakes become real bookings and real charges.

What the iPad is not good for is carrying everywhere. It stays on the desk, the kitchen table, or the bag, while the phone stays in the pocket. Use the phone for the quick question on the move, and the iPad for the session where you sit down and work through something properly. The two devices share the account, so a thread started on the phone continues on the iPad without any transfer step.

Does Voice work on iPad? What about files and Pencil?

Voice is the feature with the thinnest iPad confirmation. Muse Voice, the talking avatar, rolled out to all users in the US on November 5, 2026, and it is part of the Muse app. But Meta has not confirmed Voice separately on iPad, and no launch report describes the Voice experience on a tablet. The same gap applies to Spaces, which Meta confirmed on iOS through the App Store release notes for version 14.3, without naming iPad separately. If Voice or Spaces matters to you on iPad, check the app on your own device rather than assuming either is there.

Files are better documented. Ask for a file when the answer should be something you can keep. Documents, spreadsheets, images, and podcasts can be generated, and they are saved in the Library tab. That flow is described in iPad setup coverage, and it matches how Muse works on every surface. The iPad advantage is that the file opens at a readable size, beside the chat that produced it, instead of replacing the chat.

Apple Pencil gets one honest sentence. Reports on the iPad launch mention Apple Pencil input, but specific Pencil functions have not been detailed anywhere checked. Do not buy a Pencil expecting a Muse specific use for it. If you already own one, it works as a pointing and writing tool in the apps around Muse, and any deeper integration is unconfirmed.

Camera and image upload follow the same pattern. The iPad has a camera, and Muse accepts image uploads on the surfaces where the feature is documented, but no iPad specific camera feature was announced with version 10.0. Use the camera the way you would on a phone: photograph a document, a whiteboard, or a label, and ask Muse about it. If the iPad app handles it differently from the phone app, that difference is undocumented, and your own test is the authority.

Where is the iPad app available?

At launch, the iPad app was US only, according to multiple reports from October 7, including AppleInsider. The iPhone app already worked in Canada by then, having launched there on September 18, but the iPad reporting described the tablet app as US only for now. That was the position on launch day, and it is the last clearly dated statement about iPad countries.

Since then, Muse has launched in three more countries: Japan on October 23, Singapore on October 28, and Mexico on November 5, bringing the total to five. Meta has not published a per country list for the iPad app, and no source checked confirms whether the iPad listing is live in all five App Stores or only some. The App Store listing is a single listing covering iPhone and iPad, which suggests the tablet version follows the phone version into each country, but that is an inference from how App Store listings work, not a confirmed fact from Meta.

The practical check is the App Store on your own iPad. Open it, search for "Muse from Meta," and see what appears. If the listing offers the app, your region has it. If it does not appear at all, your region does not have it yet, and that is a rollout limit, not a fault on your device. No reinstall, no settings change, and no account change will override the region list.

If you are in a country where Muse has not launched, the iPad changes nothing. The waitlist and the honest no date position are the same as for every other device. See which countries have Muse for the current list, and the not available guide for what you can actually do today from an unlaunched country.

What if Muse does not appear on your iPad?

Check the region first. If your App Store region is a country where Muse has not launched, the app will not appear in search, and no fix on your side changes that. Check the country list, and if your country is not on it, the answer is to wait for a launch Meta has not dated, not to troubleshoot the device.

Check the iPadOS version second. The listing asks for iPadOS 18.0 or later. If your iPad runs an older version, update in Settings, under General and Software Update. If your iPad cannot run iPadOS 18, it cannot run the native Muse app, and the App Store will not offer it. That is a hardware limit, and it applies to the whole app, not just the iPad layout.

Check the listing name third. Search for "Muse from Meta," not for "Meta AI," which is a different app. The Meta AI app is the assistant inside Meta's social apps, not the Muse agent, and installing it will not give you Muse on iPad. If search shows you the wrong app, tap through to the developer page for Meta Platforms, Inc., and look for the Muse listing there.

Check the account fourth. If you use a different Apple ID on the iPad from the one on your iPhone, the App Store region follows the Apple ID signed in on the device, not the one on your phone. Sign in with the Apple ID whose region matches a launch country, if that is your situation. And if the app appears but will not install, restart the iPad and try again, which resolves the stale App Store cache that affects large apps generally.
