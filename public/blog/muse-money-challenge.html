The Muse Money Challenge: can Muse really find you $1,000

Meta chief AI officer Alexandr Wang put a number on the promise: let Muse find you $1,000 of savings. Users have posted real wins, from a $3,500 a year insurance switch to a forgotten gift card. An identity theft charity says it does not recommend the money features yet. Here is what the challenge is, what real people actually saved, and how to run it without handing over more than you mean to.

1. The short answer

As of October 25, 2026, the answer is yes, with a catch you should hear before you start. Meta chief AI officer Alexandr Wang challenged Muse users to find $1,000 of savings, and real people have posted real wins: a startup founder who found car insurance $3,500 cheaper a year, a product manager who saved $1,250 at a car dealership, a forgotten $200 Verizon gift card, and a year of book subscription payments refunded. Yahoo Finance reviewed the money features on October 22, 2026, and the Identity Theft Resource Center says it does not recommend them yet, because the technology is too new. So the challenge is real, the savings are real, and so is the risk. Muse can only find the money if you give it access to your accounts, and that access is exactly what makes the setup matter more than the challenge itself.

Here is the honest version. The $1,000 is not money Muse creates. It is money you were already losing: subscriptions you forgot, insurance you overpaid, gift cards sitting unused, bills that crept up while you were not looking. Muse is good at finding that money because it will do the boring work you never do: read every statement, compare every quote, sit in the chat queue, and ask for the refund. The catch is that the same access that lets it find the money also lets it see everything about your finances, and the same agent that can negotiate a bill can also make a mistake with your money. Then run the challenge the way this guide describes it, one account at a time, with a ceiling in writing and nothing spent without your tap.

2. What the challenge is

Muse launched in the United States and Canada on September 8, 2026, as Meta's personal AI agent: an agent that takes on work for you instead of just answering questions. Within days, users were posting about money. They were asking Muse to review their finances, cancel subscriptions, renegotiate bills, and find refunds, and some of them were coming back with numbers big enough to post about. Meta noticed, and at Meta Connect in September, Alexandr Wang turned it into a challenge. On X on September 19, 2026, he posted the line that launched it: "the last thing your unnecessary expenses see before your muse saves you $1000." The hashtag was #MuseMoneyChallenge, and the ask was simple: let Muse find you $1,000 of savings, and post what it finds.

The challenge is not a contest with a prize. There is no entry form and no winner. It is a prompt: give Muse a goal, give it access to the accounts where your money leaks, and see what it recovers. Wang has been reposting user wins since, and the thread has become the best public record of what the agent can actually do with money. Business Insider reported on September 22, 2026 that Wang was promoting Muse as able to review finances, find expenses to cut, and handle some tasks directly in the background. The pitch that made it spread was short enough to text a friend: give me access, and I will put money back in your pocket.

Wang has also been careful, in his own way, about what the challenge does not promise. In an interview with Cleo Abram for her Huge Conversations show, he said people ask Muse to make them $10,000 and that not everyone can just ask Muse to make them $10,000. "You are going to have to do your part if you want to make $10,000," he said. That distinction matters. The challenge is about finding money you already have and are losing, not about making new money. Saving $1,000 by cancelling what you forgot and renegotiating what you overpay is a realistic goal for a lot of households. Making $10,000 out of nothing is not a goal at all, and Wang has said so himself.

3. The real cases so far

The cases below are all reported by the people involved, mostly in posts on X that Wang and others reshared. They are self reported, not independently audited, and that matters: a screenshot of a saving is not a bank statement. But they are named people with specific numbers, and together they show what the challenge actually looks like in practice.

Joseph Devoy: $3,500 a year on car insurance

Startup founder Joseph Devoy said he used Muse to find car insurance that cost him $3,500 less a year than his original plan. "Killer product," he wrote while posting a screenshot of his interaction with Muse. Insurance is the biggest single win in the challenge so far, and it makes sense: car insurance prices vary wildly between providers for the same coverage, most people never compare quotes, and an agent that will pull your current policy, compare real quotes line by line, and show you the gap is doing work most people will never do themselves. The lesson is not that Muse is magic at insurance. It is that the saving was sitting there the whole time, and the only thing missing was someone willing to do the comparison.

Nick Prince: $1,250 at a car dealership

Nick Prince, a product manager at Coinbase, posted that he saved $1,250 at a car dealership when Muse helped him dodge upsells and a large fee. A dealership is a different kind of test from insurance: it is a live negotiation, in person, with a salesperson whose job is to add things to your bill. Muse helped before the visit, by preparing him for the upsells and the fee, so he could say no to them in the room. The saving here is not money Muse recovered after the fact. It is money Prince never spent, because he walked in knowing what to refuse. That is the pattern behind the best challenge wins: the agent does the homework, and the human does the saying no.

Chris Abraham: a year of book subscription refunded

One user, Chris Abraham, posted that Muse found a monthly book subscription his wife had forgotten to cancel over a year ago. "IM SICK," he wrote. "Muse just found a monthly book subscription that my wife forgot to cancel, OVER A YEAR AGO. My assistant cancelled the subscription, found their return/refund policy, and was able to get a year's worth of subscription back to me. Bonkers." This is the purest form of the challenge: money leaking every month, unnoticed, recovered in one go. It is also the case that shows what the agent actually does well: it reads the statements, spots the recurring charge nobody remembers signing up for, finds the refund policy, and asks for the money back. A human could do all of that. Almost nobody does.

The $200 Verizon gift card

Another user said Muse discovered an unused $200 Verizon gift card for them. "So it just paid for itself for the next year or two," they wrote. Gift cards are found money in the most literal sense: value you already own, sitting in an inbox or an account, doing nothing. Muse finds them because it reads across your accounts instead of inside one app at a time, and a gift card balance in a carrier account is exactly the kind of thing a cross account read turns up. It is a small win next to $3,500 of insurance, but it is also the easiest kind of win, and the kind most people can get on day one.

Armand Ruiz: $2,120.95, one piece at a time

One Meta employee, Armand Ruiz, kept a running tally of what Muse recovered for him: $2,120.95 in total, through a combination of negotiating an AT&T bill, identifying Amazon returns, returning an IKEA piece of furniture, and filing a veterinary claim. The vet claim alone was $516. The tally matters because it shows how the $1,000 target is usually reached: not in one big win, but in five or six medium ones that add up. A bill negotiated here, a return filed there, a claim chased down, a subscription cancelled. No single item is dramatic. Together they clear the bar.

There are smaller reported wins too. One user, Feifei Qiu, said she saved about $720 in an hour by moving one plan onto an unused Verizon perk and cancelling three auto renewals. The pattern is the same every time: the money was already there, the work to get it back was boring, and the agent did the boring work. None of these cases prove you will save $1,000. They prove that $1,000 of leakage is common, and that an agent willing to chase it will usually find some of it.

4. What Muse can actually do with money

The challenge works because Muse is built to act, not just to advise. Here is the list of money jobs it can take on, as Meta describes them and as users have reported them.

  • Track spending and set budgets. Connect a bank account or a card and Muse can read your spending, group it, and show you where the money actually goes. Upload statements instead if you would rather not connect the whole account. That choice matters, and section 5 explains why.
  • Audit subscriptions and flag unexpected charges. Muse reads recurring charges across your accounts, flags the ones you forgot, and finds duplicate charges. This is where most challenge wins start, because the average household juggles a dozen recurring charges it cannot fully track.
  • Negotiate bills, including by calling providers. Muse can open a live chat or call a provider, sit through the hold and the phone tree, and argue for a lower price. Our bill negotiation guide covers this job on its own, with the real cases and the failure mode to plan for.
  • Shop for insurance and compare quotes. Feed Muse your current policy and it can compare real quotes line by line instead of headline prices. The Devoy case is the headline here, and the method is the same for home insurance and other policies.
  • Search unclaimed property databases and file claims. State unclaimed property databases hold forgotten deposits, old refunds, and unclaimed payouts. Muse can search them for you and help file the claim. One user found a 401k from a 1999 employer this way. Others found small amounts, $88.92 or $48, in about five minutes of work.
  • Find deals and track prices. Muse can search for discounts, compare prices, and watch a fare or a price you care about, then tell you when it drops. One user had Muse watch a booked United fare, and when the price dropped, Muse chatted with support and came back with $1,000 in credit.
  • Chase refunds and dispute errors. Muse can read an itemized bill, question duplicate charges, find the return policy, and file the claim. One user logged Muse into a medical portal, where it questioned every line of the itemized bills, found double billings, and saved over $4,000.

Meta says purchases happen only after the user approves them, and where no card is stored the agent uses single use card numbers. Meta also says it stores passwords and payment methods so that they are hidden even from the agent, and keeps a trail of past and planned actions. Those are real safeguards, and they are the reason the challenge is runnable at all. They are not a guarantee, and Meta says so itself: the company says Muse "isn't immune to attack" and "will sometimes make mistakes." Hold both facts at once. The safeguards are real, and the mistakes are real too.

5. The counterpoint you should hear first

Yahoo Finance reviewed the money features on October 22, 2026, with Kendall Little looking at what the challenge actually involves. The review is the most careful public look at the money side of Muse so far, and it lands on the same tension as this guide: the features work best when you give Meta's software access to your bank accounts, credit cards, and other sensitive financial information, and that access is the risk. The Identity Theft Resource Center, a nonprofit that helps victims of identity theft, does not recommend the money features yet. Its position, as reported, is that the technology is too new to recommend. That is not a claim that Muse has leaked anyone's money. It is a warning that a brand new agent with deep access to your finances has not yet earned the trust that a bank or a card company has earned over decades.

The counterpoint is worth taking seriously for three reasons. First, the access is deep. To find your $1,000, Muse needs to see your statements, your subscriptions, your insurance, and your bills. That is a full picture of your financial life, held by a company whose business is advertising, not banking. Second, the agent acts. A chatbot that gives bad advice costs you a bad decision. An agent that acts on bad advice can cancel the wrong subscription, agree to the wrong price, or share the wrong detail, and the action is already done by the time you see it. Our permissions guide tells the story of exactly that failure mode, when Muse gave a seller's home address to a stranger. Third, the product is weeks old. Muse launched September 8, 2026. The money features have been live for about seven weeks as of October 25, 2026. Seven weeks is not enough time for the failure modes to be fully known, and the Matt Robb story is proof that new failure modes are still surfacing.

None of that means you should not run the challenge. It means you should run it with the access limited on purpose. You do not have to connect your whole financial life to get the value. Upload selected statements instead of connecting complete bank or credit card accounts. Start with one account, the one with the most leakage, and see what Muse finds before you add a second. Keep approvals on ask me so nothing is spent, cancelled, or agreed without your tap. And keep a human eye on the biggest items: insurance switches, large refunds, and anything that changes a policy or a contract. The challenge is a good idea run carefully. Run carelessly, it is a good way to hand a new agent the keys to your money and hope for the best.

6. How to run the challenge yourself

Run it in this order. The order is the safety system: permissions first, small wins second, big wins last.

  1. Set your permissions first. Open Muse settings, set approvals to ask me for anything that spends money, cancels a service, or shares personal details. Read our permissions guide and run the safe setup in section 5 before you connect anything financial. This is the step the challenge posts never show, and it is the step that matters most.
  2. Pick one account to start. Choose the account where the leakage lives: the card with the subscriptions, or the bank account the bills come out of. Upload a statement for that account instead of connecting the whole account, if you want the smallest possible exposure. See what Muse finds before you add anything else.
  3. Ask for the audit, not the action. Your first ask should be read only: list every recurring charge, flag anything you forgot, and show you the last three months of each one. Read the list yourself. You will recognise charges Muse cannot judge, like the subscription you actually use, and the ones you forgot, like the book subscription in the Chris Abraham case.
  4. Cancel one thing, and watch the result. Pick the clearest forgotten subscription and have Muse cancel it and ask for the refund. Check that the cancellation actually happened, and that the refund actually arrived. One clean win tells you more about the agent than ten promises.
  5. Move to bills next. Hand Muse your most annoying bill, the one that went up without explanation, and ask it to negotiate. Give it a ceiling in writing: accept any offer under a number you choose, and nothing above it without your approval. Our bill negotiation guide has the step by step and the real cases.
  6. Try insurance last. Insurance is the biggest win and the biggest risk, because switching a policy changes your coverage. Ask Muse to compare quotes line by line, and read the comparison yourself before you switch anything. A cheaper policy with worse coverage is not a saving.
  7. Search unclaimed property. This is the easiest win in the whole challenge, and it needs the least access. Ask Muse to search the unclaimed property databases for your name in every state you have lived in. Five minutes, no account connection, and sometimes real money.
  8. Keep a running tally. Write down every saving as it lands, with the date and the amount, the way Armand Ruiz did. The tally is what turns a handful of small wins into proof you cleared $1,000, and it is also your record if anything goes wrong and you need to show what Muse did.

Copy this prompt

Copy paste into Muse
I want to run the Muse Money Challenge: find me $1,000 of savings.
Rules first, and these are firm.
1. You never spend money, cancel a service, agree to a price, or share personal details without my explicit approval.
2. Start read only. Show me what you find before you act on any of it.
3. My spending ceiling for any single agreement is $0 without my approval. I approve each item one at a time.
Now do this in order.
1. Read the statement I uploaded. List every recurring charge, with the amount and the last three months of payments.
2. Flag anything that looks forgotten, duplicated, or unused, and tell me why you flagged it.
3. Pick the three biggest leaks and tell me exactly what to do about each one.
4. End with a running total of what I could recover if I act on all three, and what you need from me to start.

7. What to watch for

As of October 25, 2026, the challenge is about seven weeks old, and the honest assessment is that the wins are real and the risks are unpriced. Watch for three things as you run it. First, check every number Muse quotes against the original statement or bill before you act on it. A wrong number acted on fast is worse than a slow read you verified, and the Matt Robb story shows that agents can garble numbers in ways that read as agreements. Second, watch for the savings that are not savings: a cheaper insurance policy with worse coverage, a bill cut that came with a slower speed or a lost perk, a subscription cancelled that you actually needed. The tally should count real money kept, not numbers that look good on a screenshot. Third, watch your own behaviour. The challenge works because the agent does boring work you avoid. The risk is that you stop checking its work because the wins feel easy. The moment you start approving without reading is the moment the agent stops being an assistant and starts being a risk, and that is true whether the task is a $200 gift card or a $3,500 insurance switch.

There is also a disagreement in the sources worth naming. Meta and its supporters present the challenge as proof that an agent can pay for itself, and the user wins are the evidence. The Identity Theft Resource Center presents the same features as too new to recommend, and its caution is the evidence on the other side. Both can be right: the wins can be genuine, and the technology can still be too new to trust with full access to your finances. The way through the disagreement is the method in this guide: limited access, approvals on, small wins first, and a human read on every big item. If the challenge is worth running, it is worth running that way.
