1. The honest answer

There is no official launch date. Meta has not announced one, and anyone naming a specific date is guessing. If you see a date on social media, treat it as a rumor until Meta says it themselves.

This question gets asked so often in India because the demand is real. Muse is one of the most talked about AI apps of the year, and every major AI launch creates the same cycle: a few screenshots leak, creators post about it, and suddenly everyone wants in. That excitement is exactly what rumor pages feed on, so it helps to know what a real launch announcement looks like before the noise starts.

It also helps to understand what "launch" even means for Muse. This is not a normal app release where the store listing simply appears one morning for everyone. Muse is rolling out through an invite system, so a country launch means the rollout opens to that country: codes start circulating there, waitlist invites start converting, and access stops depending on knowing someone in a country where it already launched. Meta has not published the exact mechanics of how a new country opens up, so treat any detailed "this is how the India launch will work" post as speculation.

2. What is actually known

Muse is rolling out through an invite only system. Public reports put the start of the rollout in the US, but Meta has not published an official country list or a timeline, so anything beyond "invite only, expanding gradually" is unconfirmed. New countries open up as Meta expands the rollout, and India is one of the countries still waiting.

Here is the part that is verified, because it comes from Meta's own invite copy. When an invite code is redeemed, the referrer and the new joiner each receive 1 billion Muse tokens. A code must be redeemed in Settings within 48 hours. Tokens never expire. That is the entire confirmed rulebook. Everything else you read about pricing, dates, or regional restrictions is either unannounced or rumor.

Meta shares rollout news on their official channels. Until you see it there, nothing else counts as news, no matter how confident the post sounds. A good habit: if a claim about the launch does not link to a Meta announcement, file it under rumor and move on.

Until then, the invite system is the whole story. Codes are how people get in during the rollout, and the community pool exists so nobody has to wait on knowing the right person.

3. What launch day will actually look like

Picture the morning Meta opens India. The announcement lands on Meta's official channels first. Within hours, waitlist emails start going out, creators post their codes, and the community pool fills with fresh codes. The demand that has been building for months tries to get through the door all at once.

That surge is why preparation beats speed. On launch day itself, do three things in order. First, install the Muse app ahead of time if it is available to you, so you are not waiting on a download while codes start moving. Second, claim a code the moment you can, from the waitlist email or the community pool. Third, redeem it in Settings immediately. Do not save it for later. The 48 hour window starts when you join, and launch day codes get claimed fast.

Have your sign in ready too. Decide which account you will use before the day arrives, because fumbling between accounts while a code sits unredeemed is how people lose their window. One account, one code, one sitting.

Keep expectations realistic about the first hours as well. Invite systems often strain under launch day load. If a redeem fails once, wait a bit and try again rather than assuming the code is dead. A code that has not been redeemed is still a good code.

4. Which rumors to ignore

Fake launch dates spread fast because demand is high. The usual formats are screenshots of dates with no source, countdown pages run by strangers, YouTube thumbnails with a date stamped across them, Telegram channels selling "early access", and the classic "my friend works at Meta" claim. None of them know more than you do.

Ask where the date came from. A real launch date traces back to a Meta announcement, a post from a Meta executive, or Meta's own newsroom. If the trail ends at another rumor page, it is not a date. It is content made to get your click. This is worth repeating because the pages look convincing: clean graphics, confident language, and comments full of people asking for codes. Confidence is not sourcing.

Also ignore anyone selling early access. Meta's invite codes are free by design. When one is redeemed, both sides receive tokens, and nobody has a paid fast lane. A seller is either scamming you or charging for something they got for free.

5. How to spot a fake launch announcement

Real announcements have a boring quality to them: they come from verified Meta accounts, they link to Meta's own pages, and they never ask you for anything. Fakes do the opposite. Here are the tells.

  • It asks for payment. Invite codes are free. Any "launch" that charges an entry fee, a priority fee, or a verification fee is a scam.
  • It asks for an OTP or password. Meta will never ask for your one time password or account password to grant launch access. Anyone who does is phishing.
  • It points you to a download outside the Play Store or App Store. Sideloaded "early access" builds are a classic malware vector. Wait for the official listing.
  • It manufactures urgency. "Only 100 slots left" and "offer ends tonight" are pressure tactics. A real country launch does not work like a flash sale.
  • The account is new or unverified. Check the age of the account posting the "news" and whether it links to anything official. Fresh accounts with big claims are a red flag.

When in doubt, open Meta's official channels directly instead of clicking the link in the post. If the announcement is real, it will be there.

7. The mistakes that cost people their code

Every launch has the same casualties, and all of them are avoidable.

  • Saving the code for later. The 48 hour clock starts when you join, not when you feel ready. Redeem first, explore after.
  • Losing the code in chat history. Codes arrive in busy inboxes and crowded chats. The moment you receive one, redeem it or paste it somewhere you will find it in seconds.
  • Waiting for a personal reminder. Nobody will chase you. Set your own alert for launch news instead of assuming you will hear about it in time.
  • Signing up with an email you never open. If the waitlist email goes to an address you check twice a year, you will miss it. Use your daily inbox.
  • Trusting a seller in the rush. Launch day panic is when scams work best. Codes are free from the pool and the waitlist. Paying anyone is always the wrong move.
