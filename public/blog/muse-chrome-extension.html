What is the Muse Chrome extension?

Muse from Meta is the official Chrome extension from Meta Platforms, Inc., live on the Chrome Web Store at the extension listing. It puts Muse in Chrome's side panel, so you can chat with Muse from any page without leaving the page you are on, and pick up your existing conversations right alongside your browsing. Meta has not formally announced it. The listing was reported on November 19, 2026, and the store page itself says it was updated that day, at version 2.1.9. It had shown version 2.1.3, updated November 18, the day before, so the version moves fast and you should check the listing for the current one rather than trusting a number written anywhere else.

The extension is 2.37 MiB, in 42 languages, and the listing's own description is blunt about what it does: your personal AI agent in Chrome, hand off tasks, take busywork off your plate, and stay in control. The store page lists the work it is meant for: filling out forms, comparing prices, following up on refunds, selling a car, negotiating a bill, planning and booking a trip, tracking price drops, small business work, and money and health features. The help page that explains how Muse browses is at Meta's Help Center.

The key idea is whose browser it is. The extension runs Muse in your personal Chrome, using your own browser and your own signed in accounts. That is the opposite of the browser inside a Muse chat, which is Meta's remote browser, and the difference decides which sites work. Read the comparison section below before you choose which browser to use for a task, because the right answer depends on the site.

How do you tell the official extension from the lookalikes?

Before the install steps, one warning, because the Chrome Web Store has lookalikes and the names are close enough to confuse anyone. The official extension is called Muse from Meta, and the listing says Offered by Meta, with the developer listed as Meta Platforms, Inc., 1 Meta Way, Menlo Park, CA, and the contact email extensions@fb.com. If the listing does not say that, it is not Meta's extension. Check the name and the offered by line before you click Add to Chrome, because the lookalikes ask for the same permissions and do not come from Meta.

Two lookalikes are worth naming. Muse AI Companion is on the Chrome Web Store, by Yahor Besarab, and it is a third party extension, not Meta's. Muse AI Assistant is a different third party product, with its own install guide at museappsai.com, advertising two free requests a day and no account, and it is not Meta's either. Neither is affiliated with Meta, and neither should be confused with the official extension. The pattern is familiar: a popular product gets lookalikes within days, and the lookalikes trade on the name. The official one is the one that says Meta.

The rule to remember is simple: official means Meta Platforms, Inc. in the developer line, and the store listing at the link above. If you find a Muse extension anywhere else, or one whose developer is a person or a company you do not recognize, do not install it. The official extension is the only one that connects to your real Muse account, and it is the only one Meta supports.

How do you install it and start using it?

Install it from the Chrome Web Store. Open the listing at Muse from Meta, click Add to Chrome, and confirm. Then sign in to your Muse account. Meta also says you can ask Muse in Chat to download the Chrome extension, which gives you the same listing. Either route ends in the same place: the extension in your Chrome, signed in to your Muse account, with Muse in the side panel.

Once it is installed, the side panel is where Muse lives. Open the panel from any page, and you can pick up an existing conversation without leaving the page you are on. That is the small convenience that changes the workflow: you are reading a product page, a bill, or a booking, and Muse is beside it, not in another tab. Ask it to do the task on the page you are looking at, and watch it work in the open browser window.

Two setup choices deserve a thought before you start. First, decide which of your accounts Muse may use, because the extension can use any website you are already logged in to, without asking for login details again. If there is an account you do not want Muse to use, log out of it before you connect the extension. Second, check your web access defaults, managed in Settings, then Permissions, where you set what Muse may reach. The defaults are yours to set, and they are worth setting before the first task, not after.

What can Muse do in your Chrome?

Muse can navigate websites, control pages, help with shopping, or book cinema tickets, from the side panel. You can hand a task over entirely, or work alongside it, and you watch what it does in the open browser window. The store listing's examples are the honest shape of it: sell your car for more, negotiate a bill down, plan and book a whole trip, follow up on a refund, compare prices, track a price drop. Those are jobs that need a real browser and a real session, and that is what the extension gives it.

The shopping work is the clearest example. Muse compares prices, tracks price drops, and alerts you the moment something you want goes on sale, and it can buy on your behalf, with your OK. The small business work is the other clear example: connect your Instagram Business Account, Facebook Pages and Meta ad accounts in a few clicks, and Muse understands your business, what you sell, what your brand sounds like, and what customers keep asking about. The personal productivity work is the third: scheduling conflicts, reservations, reminders, and following up with people, so birthdays, plans and deadlines do not slip.

The money and health features are the ones to handle with care. Muse tracks expenses, audits subscriptions, flags charges you forgot about, cancels what you do not need, and helps you hit a savings goal, checking with you before it cancels or spends. As a fitness coach it builds a workout or meal plan from data you choose to share, tracks calories and nutrition, monitors sleep and wellness, and adjusts your training day by day. The store listing's own caveat applies to all of it: Muse may be inaccurate or take unexpected actions, so check in to keep it on track and intervene as needed.

What can Muse see, and what can it not see?

The privacy limits are the part of the extension worth reading carefully, because they are the opposite of what most people assume. Meta's Help Center says Muse cannot see your saved passwords, your browsing history, or your Chrome settings. Passwords on a webpage are removed from the text before Muse reads it. That is the hard limit: your saved credentials and your history are not part of what Muse can see, even when it is working inside your browser.

The tradeoffs are the part the Help Center states plainly and you should not skip. Websites Muse visits may collect information based on Muse's activity and attribute it to you. The activity may show up in your Google account and browsing history, and it may affect ads, recommendations and content on and off Meta products. That is the price of using your own browser: the sites see you, which is why they let you in, and seeing you means they can track you. If an account is one you do not want Muse to use, log out of it before you connect, because the extension will use whatever you are signed in to.

The store listing's data disclosure is the full list: personally identifiable information, health information, financial and payment information, authentication information, personal communications, location, web history, user activity, and website content. The developer declares the data is not sold to third parties, not used for purposes unrelated to the extension's core functionality, and not used to determine creditworthiness or for lending purposes. That is a broad list, and it is the honest shape of an agent that works in your browser: to do the job, it has to see the job.

How do you stay in control while Muse works?

You approve actions before they happen. That is the first control, and it is the one that matters most: Muse does not spend, cancel, or commit without your OK. The extension keeps a full activity log, and you can revoke any connector at any time. If you change your mind about a connection, you revoke it, and Muse loses that access. If you change your mind about the whole extension, you uninstall it any time from Chrome's extension settings.

To pause or take over while Muse works, click on the page, press Esc, or move the tab out of the Muse group. Any of the three stops it and hands control back to you. When Muse finishes, it gives control of Chrome back. The tabs Muse opens sit in their own group, and Muse can only take actions in those tabs, so your other tabs are not touched. That boundary is the design: the agent's workspace is the tab group, and everything outside it is yours.

The habit to build is the same one the store listing states: check in to keep it on track. Watch what Muse does in the open window, read the activity log when a task finishes, and intervene when it goes sideways. An agent in your browser is powerful because it uses your session, and it is risky for the same reason. The controls exist, and they work, but only if you use them. Do not hand over a task and walk away. Hand it over, watch the first steps, and take over the moment it looks wrong.

How is it different from the browser inside a Muse chat?

The browser inside a Muse chat is Meta's remote browser, running in a secure VM. Your credentials live in a Secure Credentials Store that the model never sees, Muse may accept essential cookies, it asks before purchases, and you watch through Open browser, pause with Take control, and stop with Stop the task. It is the safe route, and it is the route that sites increasingly block. Amazon blocked Muse outright on September 20, 2026, and other sites block agents in the same way, because they cannot tell a personal agent from a bot. The chat browser is a browser that belongs to Meta, and sites that block agents block it.

The extension is the second route. It runs in your own Chrome, with your own signed in session, so sites see you, not an agent. No fresh login is needed, because you are already logged in, and no agent block applies, because the site sees a normal browser with a normal user. That is the whole difference, and it is why the extension exists: it is the workaround for the sites that block the in chat browser. The price is the one described above: the sites can attribute the activity to you, and it may show in your history and your ads.

Poppy points in the opposite direction, and the contrast is worth one paragraph. Poppy standardizes how Muse identifies itself to a site, so the site can tell the agent from a bot and let it through. The extension works inside your own session, where the site sees the user and not the agent at all. Poppy is the agent introducing itself. The extension is the agent borrowing your identity. Both solve the same blocking problem, and they solve it in opposite ways. For now, the extension is the one that works on the sites that block agents today, and Poppy is the one that will work on the sites that adopt the standard tomorrow. The Poppy guide is at what is Poppy.

What are the limits and what is still unknown?

The limits are the ones Meta states, and they are worth taking seriously. The store listing says Muse may be inaccurate or take unexpected actions, so check in to keep it on track. That is not a disclaimer to skip. It is the operating instruction for an agent in your browser: it will make mistakes, and the mistakes are yours to catch, because it is your session, your accounts, and your money. Watch it, check the log, and take over early.

What is still unknown is the practical half. Country availability is not stated anywhere, so do not claim it works in all six Muse countries or in the US only. Price and plan requirement are not stated either: there is no price on the listing and no plan named, so do not state that it is free, even though nothing suggests a charge. User count and ratings are not settled: an early sighting showed 126 users on the listing and no ratings, and that figure is already stale. Support for other Chromium browsers, like Edge or Brave, is not confirmed by Meta, so do not claim it.

There is one date wrinkle to note and not resolve. One report dates its story November 12, 2026, while the store listing says updated November 18, with version 2.1.9 on November 19. Treat November 18 as the confirmed store date, and do not assert an earlier launch. The extension is days old, the version is moving fast, and the details will settle over the next weeks. This guide will be updated when they do.
