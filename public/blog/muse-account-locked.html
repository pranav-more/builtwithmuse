Locked out of Muse?

A lock screen, a suspension notice, or a session that drops mid task, almost always with no reason given. Here is what the three states mean, what the October 14, 2026 wave tells us, the steps that get accounts back, and what never to do.

The short answer

When Muse locks, suspends, or disconnects an account, it almost never says why. In many cases the account comes back on its own or through support, and waves of disconnections are often a platform problem rather than something the user did. On the evening of October 14, 2026, multiple Muse users reported being disconnected at roughly the same time while doing nothing unusual, a pattern that points at Meta's side, not theirs. This guide separates the three states users hit, walks through what to do in order, lists what not to do, and covers the habits that protect your codes and workflows before anything goes wrong.

What are the three states users hit?

Reports describe three distinct screens, and they are worth telling apart because the right response differs.

The lock screen

You open Muse and find the account locked, with a message that you cannot get in. One user in r/MetaAI reported waking up to this screen on October 14, 2026, their second unexplained lock in a month. Locks can be short: that same user's first lock cleared itself after about two hours, with no explanation ever given.

The suspension notice

You are told the account is suspended and directed to open a support ticket. A user in r/MuseAgent hit this on October 14, 2026 while generating an avatar and connecting a computer, and wrote that they had no idea what they had done wrong. The notice, in the reports we have seen, gives no specific reason.

The sudden disconnection

You are signed in and working, and the session simply drops. This is the quietest of the three and, during a wave, the most common. Several users described exactly this on the same October evening, which is part of why that evening matters, covered next.

One thing the three states share: none of them, in any report we have seen, arrives with a warning first. There is no yellow card. The account works until the moment it does not, which is why the recovery steps matter more than any attempt to predict the event.

How do you tell which one you have?

Read the screen, not your feelings. A lock greets you at sign in: you were out, and now you cannot get in, and the account may free itself within hours. A suspension announces itself: the word suspended appears, and the screen points you at a support ticket, which means time and process rather than patience alone. A disconnection interrupts you mid session: you were in, the session drops, and the only question is whether signing back in works. During the October wave, most reports were the third kind, which is the kind most likely to be a platform incident rather than an account decision.

What happened on the evening of October 14, 2026?

The suspension discussion in r/MuseAgent, posted on October 14, filled with matching reports. At least four other users said they had been disconnected at roughly the same time that evening while doing nothing in particular. One commenter mentioned seeing posts about the same disconnection across the wider internet that day, and a verified company post collecting reports of the problem. Nobody in the discussion had an explanation, and nobody had done anything that plausibly connected them to each other.

The reports also show what experienced users did while it happened: they checked the community first. Within hours, the thread itself had become the confirmation that the problem was shared, and the advice traded there was to wait rather than to start changing passwords, devices, and settings. That instinct is worth copying. In a wave, every change you make is a change you cannot rule out later.

The lesson is not that every lock is Meta's fault. It is that these events come in waves, and if your account drops during a wave, the odds shift strongly toward a platform incident. Before you blame your own setup, check whether other users are reporting the same thing at the same hour. Community spaces like r/MuseAgent and r/MetaAI function as the status page Meta does not publish, and a two minute check there can save you an evening of changing passwords and settings that were never the problem.

Why does Muse lock accounts without explaining?

Meta has published no criteria for locks or suspensions, and no list of behaviours that triggers them. Every user in the October reports who described the process said the same thing: no reason was given at the time, and none arrived later.

One reported path does have a shape. A user in the October 14 discussion said they saw the same lock screen after answering a Meta sign in verification email by saying the sign in was not them. Support replied the next day that the team was looking into it, and getting reconnected took a full week in that case. If you told Meta a sign in was not you, the lock that follows may be the protection working as designed, just slowly, and without telling you.

Beyond that, the honest position is that nobody outside Meta knows the triggers. Users guess at new devices, network changes, and account checks. Guesses are recorded in the forums as guesses, and this page will not promote them to facts. What is documented is the shape of the experience: it arrives without warning, it explains nothing, and it usually ends.

The missing reason has a practical cost. A user who does not know what triggered a lock cannot avoid triggering it again, and the r/MetaAI account holder locked in October was facing a second lock in a month, by their account with nothing changed in between. Until Meta publishes criteria, the only defence is the boring one in the prevention section below: consistent account signals, care with verification emails, and your valuables saved outside the account.

What should you do, step by step?

Work through these in order. Most accounts come back at step one or step two.

  1. Wait before you escalate. Short locks clear themselves; one reported lock lifted after about two hours with no action at all. Give a fresh lock a few hours, then try signing in once more.
  2. Check the email on your Meta account. A user in the October reports said an email arrived afterwards explaining what to do next. Meta's follow up message is easy to miss in a busy inbox, and it may carry the actual path back in.
  3. If the screen offers a support ticket, open it, and write it properly. Include the email address on the account, the date and time the lock or suspension started, what you were doing when it happened, and a screenshot of the exact screen. Support replied within a day in the one timeline users described in detail.
  4. While you wait, gather your records. Save your invite code, your workflow links, and anything else tied to the account that you hold outside it. If the wait stretches, you will be glad the list exists, and it takes ten minutes to make.
  5. Keep one thread with support. Reply in the same ticket rather than opening new ones, and note the dates as you go. The one detailed timeline in the reports took a week from lock to reconnection, so once a ticket is open, measure the wait in days, not hours.

One timing note, drawn from the reports rather than from any Meta promise: the visible ranges are hours for a lock that clears itself, about a day for a first reply from support, and up to a week for full reconnection when a verification answer is in dispute. Plan around those ranges. A lock that passes the one day mark without clearing belongs in a ticket, and a ticket that passes a week deserves a polite follow up in the same thread.

What should you not do?

The recovery process has its own traps, and most of them are set by panic rather than by Meta. These four are the ones the reports, and plain reasoning, warn against.

  • Do not create a fresh account to get around a lock. A new account made to sidestep a suspension can read as evasion, can compound the original problem, and starts empty: your codes, your workflows, and your history stay behind on the locked account.
  • Do not hand your password, codes, or account access to anyone offering to unlock your account. Locks create a market for scams, and no outside person can lift a Meta suspension. Anyone who says otherwise wants your account, not your recovery.
  • Do not keep hammering the sign in button. Repeated attempts do not move you up any queue, and a burst of failed sign ins can look like exactly the pattern account protection systems are built to notice.
  • Do not assume you did something wrong. The October 14 wave dropped accounts that were doing nothing at all. Silence from Meta is not a verdict on you.

How do you lower the odds, and the cost?

Nothing makes an account immune, because some of these events are waves that have nothing to do with you. But a few habits reduce both the chance of a self inflicted lock and the damage when one lands.

Keep your account signals consistent

The country settings on your Meta account matter more than most users realise. In the wider availability discussions, users locked out while living in a launched country suspect a mismatch between the country on their account and where they actually use Muse. That is a suspicion drawn from user reports, not a confirmed cause, but keeping your account country, your payment details, and your actual location consistent costs nothing. Our guide to using Muse where it has not launched covers the country side in full.

Be careful with the words not me

When Meta emails to ask whether a sign in was you, answer honestly, and understand the trade. Saying it was not you is the right call when it truly was not, and it may still cost you a lock and a week of support while Meta sorts out whose sign in it was, as one user reported in October. It is protection with a slow fuse, not a punishment. Just do not tap it as a reflex when the sign in was yours and you simply do not remember it.

Keep your valuables outside the account

Save your invite code and the links to your published workflows somewhere that does not need a Muse sign in: a note, an email to yourself, anywhere boring. A lock blocks the account, not the code itself, but you cannot reach anything stored only inside. Our risk checklist makes the same point for business accounts: the time to copy your essentials out is before you need them. And if your code came from the community pool, our referral code guide explains where codes live and how they move between people.

The ten minute backup

Concretely, save these four things today, outside the account: the text of your invite code; the links to any workflows you have published; the email address the account is registered to; and a screenshot of your profile page. That is the whole backup. It fits in one note, it takes ten minutes, and it converts any future lock from a small disaster into an inconvenience with a paper trail. If the worst happens and support asks what was on the account, you will be the rare user who can simply show them.

Frequently asked questions

Why did Muse suspend my account?
In most reported cases, no reason is ever given. Some suspensions arrive in waves that drop many accounts at once, which points to a platform event rather than anything the user did. One reported path is answering a sign in verification email by saying the sign in was not you.
How long does a Muse account lock last?
Reports range from about two hours, for a lock that cleared itself, to a full week, for a case that went through support. Meta publishes no timeline. Open a ticket if a lock lasts beyond a day, and expect the wait to be measured in days.
How do I contact Muse support?
The suspension and lock screens direct you to open a support ticket, which is the route users describe using. Include the email on your account, when it happened, what you were doing, and a screenshot of the screen. In the one detailed timeline reported, support replied the next day.
Can I make a new Muse account while mine is locked?
You can try, but you should not. A fresh account made to get around a lock can read as evasion and compound the problem, and it starts empty, without your codes or workflows. Recovering the original account is almost always the better path.
I said a sign in was not me and then got locked. Is that connected?
It can be. A user reported seeing the same lock screen right after answering a Meta sign in verification email that way, and reconnection took a week through support. The lock looks like the protection working slowly, not a punishment, but Meta does not confirm the link.
Will I lose my invite code and workflows while my account is locked?
A lock blocks signing in, so everything inside the account is out of reach until it lifts. No report we have seen shows codes or workflows being deleted by a lock itself. Saving your code and workflow links outside the account means a lock costs you time, not property.
