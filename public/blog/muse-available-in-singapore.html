1. The status: not available in Singapore yet

No. As of October 25, 2026, Muse has not launched in Singapore, and Meta has not announced a launch date. That is the whole answer, and it has not changed since Muse first appeared. If you are reading from Singapore, you cannot sign up for Muse in your own country yet, and no date on any site can tell you when that changes, because Meta has published none.

The full list is short. Muse launched in the United States on September 8, 2026, reached Canada on September 18, 2026, and arrived in Japan on October 23, 2026, the first country outside North America to get it. Those three countries are the complete list as of October 25, 2026. Everywhere else, including Singapore, Muse has not launched yet. We keep the full list of Muse available countries current, and it says the same thing: Singapore is not on it yet.

That puts Singapore in a strange spot. It is one of the first places global technology products usually reach in Asia, its people were among the fastest to try every new AI product of the last two years, and Meta's own hardware reached Singapore before most of the world. None of that is a launch. A launch is an announcement with a date, an app listing in the Singapore App Store and on Google Play, and a sign in that works from a Singapore account. None of those three things exists yet, so the honest status is simple: not yet, no date.

This guide stays with what is known. It covers why Singapore keeps coming up in every conversation about where Muse goes next, what the Japan launch tells us about the order of things, what to do while you wait, what to prepare now so the first day works, and the warnings that save you from fake dates and fake codes. Nothing here invents a date, and nothing here pretends the wait is over.

2. Why Singapore keeps coming up

Singapore is one of the two countries readers ask us about most since Japan launched, and the question is always the same: Japan got it, so when do we get it? The question is fair. Singapore is the place where global launches in Asia usually start, English is the working language of the whole country, and adoption of new technology runs ahead of almost everywhere else in the region. When a product like Muse opens in one Asian country and not the next, the next country asks first, and Singapore is asking loudest. The other country readers ask about most is South Korea, and our South Korea guide gives the same honest status for readers there.

The confusion in Singapore has a specific cause, and it is worth clearing up first. Meta's AI glasses went on sale in Singapore in September, one of the first markets anywhere to get them, and Meta has said Muse will come to its glasses. Plenty of people in Singapore saw the glasses launch and assumed the agent had launched with them. It had not. The glasses and the agent are two different rollouts, and the agent has not arrived in Singapore on any device, glasses included. If you bought the glasses in Singapore, you have the hardware. You do not have Muse.

The same pattern showed up in South Korea and Mexico, where the glasses also went on sale in September without the agent, so Singapore is not a special case and it is not being skipped on purpose. Meta simply sells its hardware in more places than its agent runs in, and the gap between the two lists is where most of the confusion lives. The way to tell the difference is simple: hardware has a price and a shop page, while the agent has an announcement with a country name in it. Singapore has the first and not the second.

There is also a practical reason Singapore matters so much as a next launch. It is the regional base for most global technology companies in Asia Pacific, it is where many product teams sit, and it is where a launch reaches the whole region's press, developers and early users in a single day. A Singapore launch would not just serve Singapore, it would put Muse in front of the people who build with new tools for a living across the region. That is a reason to expect Singapore early in the rollout, and it is still not a date, because Meta has published no order, no list, and no timetable for any country after Japan.

So the status for Singapore is the same as for every country still waiting: wanted, watched, and undated. The demand is real, the hardware is already here, and the agent is not. Everything below is what to do with that wait.

3. What the Japan launch tells us about what happens next

Japan is the closest thing Singapore has to a preview, because Japan is the only launch since the first two, and it shows how Meta moves. The pattern so far is one country at a time, about five weeks apart at the start: the United States on September 8, Canada on September 18, then a pause, then Japan on October 23. Meta announced Japan on the day itself, through its Japanese newsroom and the official Muse account, and the app went live in the Japanese App Store and on Google Play the same day. There was no advance date, no public countdown, and no list of next countries. Japan simply arrived.

That pattern cuts both ways for Singapore. On the hopeful side, Japan proves the rollout has left North America, and Asia is now open for launches. More than ten million people set up Muse in the United States and Canada in its first weeks, and Japan is the first test of whether the agent travels outside North America at all. Japan also proves Meta is willing to launch with local services from day one: Gmail and Google Calendar worked at launch, Klook travel bookings worked from launch day, and Meta named Mercari and Money Forward ME as connections on the way. A Singapore launch, when it comes, will very likely look the same, with global connectors first and local services following. Our guide to using Muse in Japan shows exactly what that first day looked like, and it is the best picture available of what a first day in Singapore would look like too.

On the cautious side, Japan proves nothing about timing. Meta gave no warning before Japan, and it has given no warning since. There is no published order, no public list, and no statement that Singapore is next. Japan arrived about five weeks after Canada, but five weeks is not a schedule, it is a gap between two events, and gaps do not repeat on command. Anyone who tells you Singapore launches in a particular month is guessing, and the guess is worth exactly what you paid for it.

The useful lesson from Japan is about preparation, not prediction. In Japan, the people who got the most from day one were the people who already had the pieces in place: a Meta account, a Gmail account they actually used, a Google Calendar with their real schedule in it, and a clear idea of the first tasks they wanted Muse to handle. The launch itself took minutes. The preparation took longer, and it is the part you can do now, from Singapore, before any announcement exists.

5. What to prepare now so day one works

Waiting is not doing nothing. The preparation below takes an afternoon, it costs nothing, and it is the difference between a launch day that works and a launch day spent fixing accounts. Every item here is something that worked from day one in the United States, Canada and Japan, so every item here is a safe bet for Singapore.

  • A Meta account you can sign in to. Muse needs a Meta account, and you can create one with an email address or a phone number. Make it now, sign in once, and make sure you can get back in. A launch day is a bad day to discover you forgot a password.
  • A Gmail account you actually use. Gmail is the connector that makes Muse useful on day one, and it was live in every launch country from the start. If your mail lives somewhere else, move the mail that matters, or set up Gmail forwarding, so the inbox Muse sees is the inbox where your work arrives. Our Gmail connector guide covers the connection itself and the one account limit, so read it before launch day and you will connect in minutes.
  • A Google Calendar with your real schedule. Muse reads your calendar to plan your day, spot clashes, and remind you what needs prep. A calendar with three test events in it teaches you nothing. Put your real work, your real meetings, and your real deadlines in it now, and the first briefing Muse gives you will be about your actual life. Our Google Calendar connector guide covers how the pairing with Gmail works.
  • A short list of first tasks. Write down five things you would ask Muse to do in its first week: the morning inbox summary, the calendar check, the trip plan, the form you keep putting off, the research question you never get around to. A list stops you staring at a blank chat box on day one, and it tells you fast whether the agent is doing what you hoped.
  • The privacy setting, decided in advance. Decide now that the first thing you will do inside the app is open Settings, then Data controls, and turn off Help improve our AI models. That switch controls whether Meta may use your chats to train future models. Deciding now means you do it before you share anything real, instead of remembering it a week later.
  • Approvals set to ask you first. Before you connect anything, set approvals so nothing is sent or paid for without your tap. This is the single most important safety step on the page, and it matters more the more useful the agent becomes. An agent that can send email and book things is only safe when it checks with you first.

None of this needs Muse to exist in Singapore. A Meta account works today, Gmail works today, Google Calendar works today, and a list of tasks works on paper. Do the preparation now, and the launch becomes a five minute job: download, sign in, connect, ask.

6. What will work on day one

Here is what a first day in Singapore will very likely look like, based on what day one looked like in the three countries that already have Muse. The basic version is free, including the app download and the core features. You can talk to Muse by text or by voice, and you can ask it to carry out tasks in steps, not just answer questions. It will keep working in the background after you close the app, and it will ask for your approval before it sends an email or pays for anything. You need to be 18 or older to use it.

Gmail and Google Calendar will almost certainly be there from the start, because they were live in the United States, Canada and Japan from launch day, and they are the two connectors every launch has shared. Connect them and Muse can summarise your unread email, manage your schedule, and keep both current as you talk to it. If you did the preparation in the section above, this step takes minutes, and the first morning briefing you get will be the moment the product stops being a demo and starts being a routine.

Paid subscription plans will also exist for people who want more usage, the same pattern as in the other launch countries. Meta did not publish Japan specific prices in its launch announcement, and there is no reason to expect Singapore prices to be published in advance either, so check the plan screen inside the app before you subscribe to anything. Nothing about the free tier requires a card, and you can use Muse properly before you ever see a plan screen. The free tier is the full starting point, and it is where everyone should begin.

What about Singapore services? Nothing is announced. Japan launched with Klook from day one and Mercari and Money Forward ME named as coming soon, which shows Meta builds local lineups country by country. Singapore has its own services at the center of daily life, from travel booking to food delivery, and a Singapore launch would need its own lineup, but Meta has named none of them, and no Singapore company has announced a Muse connection. Expect the global connectors first, watch for local ones after, and treat any list of Singapore launch partners you see before an announcement as invention. Travel is the one area worth watching early, because Klook, which worked from day one in Japan, is a Singapore company, and travel bookings were part of the Japan launch from the start.

One more thing to expect on day one: an invite code screen. The download is public, and access in the launch countries still runs on invite codes, so many members are asked for one during setup. That is exactly what the waitlist is for. Have the code ready, and the screen is a ten second step instead of a dead end.

7. What does not work yet, and the warnings that matter

The honest warnings, in one place, so you do not waste time or money on things that cannot work yet.

  • There is no Muse app in the Singapore stores. Search the App Store or Google Play in Singapore and you will not find Muse from Meta. The listing appears in a country on launch day, not before. Any app with a similar name is not Muse, and installing it will not get you in.
  • There is no launch date, anywhere. Not on Meta's newsroom, not on muse.ai, not in any Singapore newspaper, not in any leak worth trusting. Meta publishes no country list and no expansion timeline. A date with no source is a guess, and a date with a source that is not Meta is still a guess.
  • The glasses are not the agent. Meta's AI glasses went on sale in Singapore in September, and Meta has said Muse will come to its glasses. Owning the glasses does not give you Muse, and wearing them does not change the status on this page.
  • No Singapore service is connected yet. No Singapore bank, shop, map, chat app or delivery service has announced a Muse connection, and Meta has named none. If a Singapore service claims it works with Muse today, ask for the announcement, because there is none.
  • Paid codes and paid early access are scams. Invite codes are free from the community pool and from the waitlist. Anyone charging money for a Singapore code, a Singapore account, or a way around the wait is selling air. Report the listing and move on.
  • Watch the source, not the headline. Every launch so far was announced by Meta itself, on the day, through its newsroom or the official Muse account. Until Meta says it, it has not happened, whatever a video thumbnail promises.

None of this is meant to put you off. It is meant to keep the wait cheap. The people who waste the most time waiting are the people chasing dates that do not exist and paying for access that cannot work. The waitlist is free, the preparation is free, and the launch email is free. Everything else is noise.
