How to Use the Google Tasks Connector in Muse

Connect Google Tasks to Muse and your to dos stop living in your head. Tell Muse what needs doing and it lands on a list, with a due date, where you will actually see it. Here is how to connect it and plan a day from it.

1. What is the short answer?

As of October 25, 2026, Google Tasks is a live connector in Muse. It was in the list of connectors inside the Muse app by September 29, 2026, and it was still there in the October 15 check, which counted 54 connectors in the web app. That September sighting is the latest dated source for this connector, so treat the status as live since late September with no October change reported. Connect it and Muse can read your to do lists, add tasks from a chat, set due dates, and check things off when they are done. It turns the sentence "remind me to do this" into a task on a list you will actually see again.

Here is the honest version. Google Tasks is the simplest connector in the Google set, and simple is the point. A to do list only works if adding to it takes less effort than remembering the task in your head, and talking to Muse is the lowest effort way to add a task that exists. The value is not a fancy project system. It is that the tasks stop living in your head and start living on a list with dates on them. The rest of this guide covers how to connect it, how to plan a day from your mail and calendar, the uses that are worth your time, and the limits to know before you lean on it.

2. What do you need?

  • Muse access. You need an account with access first, in the United States, Canada, or Japan, where Muse has launched.
  • A Google account. The connector reads the task lists in the Google account you sign in with, the same account that holds your Gmail and Google Calendar.
  • Your Google sign in. You connect with your Google account, the same way you sign in to Gmail itself.
  • Google Calendar connected, or at least planned. Tasks tell you what to do. Calendar tells you when you have time to do it. Our Google Calendar connector guide covers that half.
  • Gmail connected, optional but recommended. Most tasks arrive as email. Tasks plus Gmail lets Muse turn a message into a task without you retyping it. Our Gmail connector guide covers that half.

3. How do you connect it?

The connection itself takes a few minutes. The setup around it matters more, so run the steps in order and do not skip the step where you name your lists. Our complete connectors list keeps the dated status of all 54 live connectors in the web app, plus the 18 announced but not live, so check there if a connector you expect is missing from your account.

  1. Open Muse and go to Settings. The connectors list lives there. What is printed there is what your account has, because rollout is staged and two accounts can see different lists on the same day.
  2. Find Google Tasks in the connectors list and open it. If Google Tasks is not listed, your account has not received it yet. There is nothing to install and nothing you broke. Check again in a few days.
  3. Sign in with Google. Use the Google account that holds the task lists you actually use. This is the account Muse will read and add to, so sign in with the right one the first time.
  4. Grant the access Muse asks for. Muse needs permission to read and change your tasks. Read the permission screen before you accept it. If a scope surprises you, stop and check what it covers before continuing.
  5. Name your lists before you use them. In Google Tasks, make one list for work, one for personal, and one for anything you are waiting on from other people. Three lists are enough. Ten lists are a filing system you will never open.
  6. Test with one real task. Ask Muse to add a task with a due date, then open Google Tasks and check it landed on the right list with the right date. If it matches, the connection is good.
  7. Use it by voice for a week. Every time a task pops into your head, tell Muse instead of trusting your memory. At the end of the week, count how many tasks you captured that you would otherwise have lost.

You are my task manager. Use my Google Tasks connector, and my Gmail and Google Calendar connectors when they help.
Rules: you never mark a task done unless I say it is done. You never delete a task unless I say delete it. You add tasks only to the list I name, or to my work list if I name no list.
Every morning, do this in order.
1. Show me my tasks due today, in order of due time, and my overdue tasks at the top.
2. Check my Gmail for anything new that needs doing and suggest it as a task. Ask me before you add it.
3. Check my calendar and tell me which tasks fit in the free time I have today.
4. End with the 3 tasks I should do first, in order.
When I tell you about a new task in chat, add it to the right list with a due date if I give one, and read it back to me so I can check it.

4. What can you do with it?

The connector earns its place with six uses. Everything else is a variation on one of them. None of them needs a project management degree. All of them work because the list is where the tasks live, and Muse is the fastest way to get them there.

Capture a task the second you think of it

Most tasks are lost in the ten seconds between thinking of them and finding somewhere to write them down. Tell Muse in plain words and the task lands on your list with a date on it. "Add a task to call the plumber tomorrow" is the whole workflow. No app to open, no form to fill, no list to find. The habit to build is telling Muse immediately, every time, instead of trusting yourself to remember it later. You will not remember it later. Nobody does. The test at the end of week one is simple: open your list and count the tasks you captured in passing that would otherwise have vanished. That count is the value of the connector, and it is usually bigger than people expect.

Turn email into tasks without retyping

Most tasks arrive as email: a client asking for a quote, a supplier asking for approval, a colleague asking for a document. With Gmail connected, ask Muse to read your mail and suggest the tasks hidden in it, then approve the ones worth adding. Our Gmail connector guide covers the mail half. The rule that makes this safe is that Muse suggests and you approve. A task Muse added on its own from a mail thread you never read is a commitment you never made. Ask Muse to show you the task and the message it came from, approve the good ones, and skip the rest. Do this every morning and your list starts the day already knowing what the inbox asked of you.

Plan the day from tasks, mail, and calendar together

This is the use that makes the connector more than a list app. Ask Muse for your tasks due today, then ask which ones fit in the free time your calendar shows, and which ones the morning mail just made urgent. Our Google Calendar connector guide covers the calendar half, and our small business morning briefing guide builds the same habit for owners, with mail, calendar, and tasks in one briefing. The plan that comes back is not a list of everything due. It is a short list of what fits today, in order, with the rest moved to a day where it can actually happen. A task without a day attached to it is a wish. A task with a day and a free hour attached to it is a plan.

Keep a waiting list for other people

Half of a working day is waiting: waiting for a client to approve, waiting for a supplier to reply, waiting for a colleague to send a file. Make one list called Waiting and put every task that depends on someone else on it, with the date you asked. Once a week, ask Muse to show you the waiting list and flag anything that has sat for more than a few days. Those are your follow ups for the week. This is the use that quietly recovers work you had already won and then forgot, because the threads you dropped are usually the ones closest to done. The rule is simple: a promise you are waiting on is still open until the other person answers, and the person you are waiting on does not know you are waiting unless you chase them.

Break big work into small tasks

A task that says launch the new website is not a task. It is a project wearing a task costume, and it will sit on your list untouched because nobody knows where to start. Ask Muse to break it into small steps: write the homepage copy, pick the photos, set up the contact form, test it on a phone. Each step is a task you can actually do in one sitting, and checking one off moves the project forward in a way you can see. The test for a good task is whether you could start it right now without thinking. If you cannot, it is still too big. Break it again.

Close the day with what got done

At the end of the day, ask Muse what you completed today and what is still open for tomorrow. The completed list matters more than it sounds. Most people end the day remembering only what they did not finish, and that is a miserable way to measure a day. Seeing the finished tasks, counted, changes the feeling of the day and shows you what your time actually went on. Then move the open tasks to a day with room for them, instead of letting them sit overdue and turn red. An overdue task you keep carrying is a small daily failure notice. A task you deliberately moved to Thursday is a plan.

5. What can it not do?

The limits are honest and worth knowing before you lean on the connector.

  • It cannot run a project for you. Google Tasks is a list, not a project system. There are no boards, no assignees, no dependencies, and no progress reports. If you need those, you need a different tool.
  • It cannot tell you what matters most. Muse can order tasks by due date and by the free time you have, but only you know which task actually moves your work forward. A due date is not the same as importance.
  • It cannot do the task. Adding "call the client" to a list does not call the client. The connector captures and organizes. The doing is still yours.
  • It cannot see tasks in other apps. It reads Google Tasks only. Tasks in another to do app, in a notes app, or on paper stay invisible unless you move them into Google Tasks.
  • It cannot stop you overloading a day. If you add twelve tasks to a day with two free hours, Muse can tell you they do not fit. It cannot stop you adding the thirteenth. Saying no to a task is still your job.

6. What should you watch for?

As of October 25, 2026, Google Tasks is a quiet, settled connector: in the app list by September 29, still listed in the October 15 check of 54 connectors in the web app, with no October change reported. There is no new feature to chase here, so the habits around it still matter. The first habit is checking the list every morning. A task list you never open is no better than a memory, and a list you open once a week is a list of stale good intentions. Open it daily, even for two minutes, and it stays honest.

The second habit is keeping lists small. Three lists are enough: work, personal, and waiting. The moment you have a list for every project and every area of life, you have built a filing system that takes longer to maintain than the tasks take to do. If a list has more than twenty open tasks, it is not a list any more. It is a pile. Move the tasks that cannot happen this week off it, give the rest a date, and let the pile shrink.

The third habit is the due date rule. A task with no date is a task that never happens. When you add a task, give it a date, even a rough one. If you genuinely do not know when it should happen, give it next Friday and move it if you must. A moved date is a decision. No date is a shrug. Check your data controls too. In Muse settings, open Data controls and look at the switch for helping improve the models. If you do not want your chats used for training, turn it off before you start asking Muse to manage your tasks. That switch controls whether Meta may use your chats to train future models.

Finally, remember what this connector is and what it is not. It is the simplest way to capture and organize to dos, and it shines when Gmail and Calendar are connected alongside it, because tasks, mail, and time are one system, not three. It is not a project manager, a team tool, or a substitute for deciding what matters. Use it for the capture and the daily plan, and let the bigger planning live where it belongs: in your head, with a list that tells you the truth about the day. Frequently asked questions

What does the Google Tasks connector do in Muse?
It lets Muse read your Google Tasks lists, add tasks from a chat, set due dates, and check tasks off when they are done. Tell Muse what needs doing and it lands on a list with a date on it.
Is Google Tasks live in Muse now?
Yes. It was in the connectors list inside the Muse app by September 29, 2026, and it was still listed in the October 15 check. That September sighting is the latest dated source, and no October change has been reported.
Can Muse add tasks from my email?
Yes, when Gmail is connected too. Ask Muse to read your mail and suggest the tasks hidden in it, then approve the ones worth adding. Muse suggests, you approve, and nothing is added without your say.
Can Muse mark my tasks done?
Only when you say a task is done. Set that rule in your prompt and Muse will never mark a task complete on its own. A task marked done by mistake is a task you will forget entirely.
Is Google Tasks a project management tool?
No. It is a simple to do list with lists, due dates, and check boxes. If you need boards, assignees, and dependencies, you need a different tool, and Muse can help you use that one separately.
Can I use the Google Tasks connector outside the United States, Canada, and Japan?
Not yet. Muse itself has launched in the United States, Canada, and Japan only. Join the waitlist and we will send you a fresh code on launch day in your country.
